# Transition Skeleton — At-Home Assignment

**Due:** Sunday, Nov 8, 2026, at 11:59 p.m.

## Purpose

This assignment uses your draft's transitions to test its reasoning. Every transition makes a claim about how two ideas relate ("however" claims contrast, "therefore" claims consequence, "also" claims only that one more thing is coming). A transition lives at the seam between paragraphs: the last sentence of one paragraph says what it has established, and the first sentence of the next says where the argument goes from there. Pulled out and read in sequence, those seams should work as a skeleton of your argument. Where the skeleton breaks, the argument usually does too. The aim is to move your essay away from a list of reasons (three or four parallel points that could appear in any order) and toward a train of thought, in which each paragraph exists because of what the previous one established.

## Steps

1. **Extract.** Copy into a numbered list, in order, each "joint" in your draft: the last sentence of a paragraph paired with the first sentence of the paragraph that follows. Start with the last sentence of your introduction and end with the first sentence of your conclusion. Also note any sentence inside a paragraph where the direction of thought shifts.
2. **Read the skeleton.** Read the list on its own, top to bottom, as if it were the whole essay. Mark any point where you, as a reader, would ask "how did we get here?" or "why this next?" Then ask whether any two body paragraphs could swap places without loss. If they could, the essay is still listing reasons.
3. **Label the claim.** For each joint, write the relationship it currently claims between the two paragraphs (addition, elaboration, example, cause, contrast, concession, escalation, conclusion). Then write the relationship you actually mean. Note too whether each closing sentence states what its paragraph established, or simply trails off on the last piece of evidence or quotation.
4. **Apply the "and also" test.** For each joint, ask whether the opening sentence could begin with "and also" without changing the meaning. If so, the two paragraphs are in list relation. In this assignment, list relation is what you are revising away from. For each such joint, ask what the previous paragraph's conclusion makes possible, necessary, or problematic, and let the answer become the next step. Useful questions: What does this point leave unexplained? What objection does it invite? What does it imply that I haven't said yet?
5. **Revise at least three joints.** Choose the three weakest (any that failed step 4 are good candidates) and rewrite both sentences: the closing sentence should state what the paragraph has established, and the opening sentence should (a) pick that up, (b) name where the next paragraph goes, and (c) state why the second follows from the first.
6. **Follow the problem into the draft.** If you could not write a strong joint somewhere, that is a finding, not a failure. Decide what it reveals: the paragraphs need reordering, one should be cut, a step is missing between them, or your thesis needs adjusting. Make that change in the draft.

Example of a revision for step 5:

- _Before:_ [closing] ends on a block quotation from the speech, with no comment. [opening] "Douglass also talks about the hypocrisy of the churches."
- _After:_ [closing] "For Douglass, then, the nation's celebration is not merely incomplete but self-condemning." [opening] "That charge could be dismissed as political grievance; by turning next to the churches, Douglass extends the indictment to the institution that claimed moral authority over politics itself."

## What to submit and specs

Submit one document with three parts:

1. **The skeleton:** your numbered list of original joints (closing sentence + opening sentence), each labeled with the relationship it claims and the relationship you mean (steps 1–4).
2. **The revisions:** at least three before/after joints (step 5), plus a sentence or two on any change you made to the draft itself (step 6).
3. **Reflection (150–250 words):** What did reading the skeleton on its own show you about your argument that reading the full draft did not? Where did your essay list reasons, and how did revising the joints turn that list into a sequence of thought?
