# Week 2 — Sentence Imitation: Parataxis and Hypotaxis

**Due Sunday August 30th at Midnight :** two labeled examples from this week's reading, plus three original sentences written twice each — once paratactically and once hypotactically — and a short reflection. No more than one page.

## What this means

Coordination and subordination are the two basic ways English marks the relationship between clauses. This exercise asks you to feel the difference by writing the same content both ways.

**Parataxis** places clauses side by side as equals. Each clause could stand alone as a complete sentence; they're joined by coordinating conjunctions (_and, but, or, so, yet, for_) or simply set beside one another with a comma or semicolon. Parataxis doesn't tell you how the clauses relate — it just adds them, one after another, and leaves you to infer the connection. It's the syntax of the King James Bible ("And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep"). Rowlandson's narrative opens in this mode:

> "On the tenth of February 1675, came the Indians with great numbers upon Lancaster: their first coming was about sunrising; hearing the noise of some guns, we looked out; several houses were burning, and the smoke ascending to heaven."

Four clauses, none ranked above another, separated by a colon and semicolons that assert no logic at all. The sentence registers what happened in the order it was perceived. It offers no explanation, and no interpretation.

**Hypotaxis** ranks its clauses. One clause is grammatically independent; the rest are dependent, introduced by a subordinating conjunction (_because, although, since, while, if, when, that_) or a relative pronoun (_who, which, that_) that states outright how the parts relate — as cause, purpose, concession, condition, or time. Hypotaxis makes the logic explicit instead of implied. Rowlandson turns to this mode in the Second Remove, when she stops reporting events and begins interpreting them:

> "But the Lord renewed my strength still, and carried me along, that I might see more of His power; yea, so much that I could never have thought of, had I not experienced it."

The subordinating words do the interpretive work here: _that I might see_ makes her survival purposive rather than accidental, _so much that_ measures the result against a cause, and the inverted conditional _had I not experienced it_ marks the whole claim as knowledge available only through suffering. This is worth noticing as a pattern. Where Rowlandson reports, she tends toward parataxis; where she reads events as providence, she reaches for subordination. The syntax is doing theological work.

**What changes when you recast:** here is that same sentence made paratactic —

> The Lord renewed my strength, and He carried me along, and I saw more of His power, and I could never have imagined so much.

The events are identical. What disappears is the frame. The paratactic version leaves you to decide whether God strengthened her _in order that_ she might see His power, or whether she simply saw it afterward — the very question the hypotactic original settles.
