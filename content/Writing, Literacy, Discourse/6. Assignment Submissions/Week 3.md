# Week 3 — Subordinating Paragraphs

**Due:** Monday Sept. 7th at Midnight, double spaced 12 pt. font.

**Two items** — (1) a subordinate paragraph you find in the Edwards reading labeled by level (L1, L2, etc.) with a brief note on what move each level makes on the one before it; and (2) a paragraph of your own — a topic sentence (Level 1) followed by three levels of subordinate sentences (Levels 2–4), the whole reading as one coherent paragraph, not four separate sentences.

**What this means:**

This exercise builds a paragraph the way the sentence exercise built a sentence, one level of specificity at a time. But the numbering isn't just sequence — it's a claim about logical relationship. Each level doesn't just come after the one above it; it is generated by it, in one of a few ways: it can restate the prior claim more concretely, supply the mechanism or evidence for it, or narrow its scope. If you can delete a sentence and the paragraph still makes the same point with the same force, that sentence wasn't really subordinate — it was just added.

- **Level 1** is your topic sentence — the most general statement, the claim the whole paragraph exists to support.
- **Level 2** develops Level 1 directly — it should answer "how so?" or "in what way?" about L1, not introduce a new but related idea.
- **Level 3** develops Level 2 the same way — it earns its place by explaining or particularizing L2, not by returning to L1.
- **Level 4** develops Level 3 — by this level you should be at the most concrete, specific point the paragraph reaches.

A useful test: read only L1 and L4 together. If L4 sounds like a wild non sequitur next to L1, the chain in between is doing its job — it's the only thing making that jump legible. If L4 could stand next to L1 with no bridge needed, the levels weren't really subordinating.

**Note on why this matters for argumentative writing:** This is not just a sentence-craft drill. Subordination — each unit justifying, qualifying, or specifying the one above it — is the basic structural logic of an argument, not just of a paragraph. Argumentative prose tends to resist purely _cumulative_ structure (where sentences add loosely related, coordinate details at roughly the same level of generality, the way descriptive or narrative prose often does) because an argument's job is to keep narrowing toward a warranted, specific claim, not to accumulate texture around a general one. Recognizing the difference between "adding" and "subordinating" is, in miniature, the difference between description and argument.

**Example of Cumulative Paragraph:**

- L1 — The city was dying.
- L2 — Its streets, once crowded, stood empty by noon.
- L2 — Shop windows, dusty and dark, reflected no one walking past.
- L2 — Only a stray cat paused there, sniffing at a torn awning before moving on.

Notice this example is cumulative not subordinate — each level adds a coordinate concrete detail rather than logically entailing the one above it. That's fine for description. Watch how the second example differs.

**Example of a subordinating structure (Edwards, "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God"):**

- L1 — "That the reason why they are not fallen already, and don't fall now, is only that God's appointed time is not come."
- L2 — "For it is said that when that due time, or appointed time comes, their foot shall slide."
- L3 — "Then they shall be left to fall, as they are inclined by their own weight."
- L4 — "God won't hold them up in these slippery places any longer, but will let them go; and then, at that very instant, they shall fall to destruction; as he that stands in such slippery declining ground on the edge of a pit that he can't stand alone, when he is let go he immediately falls and is lost."

L2 supplies the scriptural warrant for L1's claim about timing; L3 draws out the practical consequence once that warrant is met; L4 renders the fall itself in the sermon's characteristic image, at the most concrete point the passage reaches.

**Instructions:**

1. Find one more example of a subordinate paragraph from Edwards and label its sentences L1 through L4 (or however many levels it has, it can have more, but each new sentence should represent a new level) briefly noting in a phrase or two what kind of move each level makes on the one before it (restatement, evidence, mechanism, narrowing).
2. Write one topic sentence (Level 1) of your own.
3. Write a Level 2 sentence that develops the topic sentence directly.
4. Write a Level 3 sentence that develops the Level 2 sentence.
5. Write a Level 4 sentence that develops the Level 3 sentence.
6. A description of how each of your sentences develops from the previous one's along the same lines of your analysis of the Edwards paragraph.

---

_Don't just clear the bar. A four-sentence paragraph that technically subordinates correctly but plays it safe teaches you less than one where you push the logic somewhere genuinely difficult to sustain — try a topic sentence abstract enough that getting to L4 without breaking the chain is actually hard. This is a low-stakes place to test how far you can carry a line of reasoning before it snaps; use it that way._
