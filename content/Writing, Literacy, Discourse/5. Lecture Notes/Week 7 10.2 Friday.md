**Why this class asks you to struggle (and why AI undercuts that)**

- This class is about learning a skill, and the learning happens where the work is hardest. Like practicing an instrument or a sport, you get better by doing the parts you can't do yet. If a chatbot does those parts for you, you skip the learning.
- Drafts in this class are _supposed_ to be messy and incomplete. Individual assignments aren't graded. Only the revised final portfolio counts, and it's judged on effort, revision, and growth. Nothing you hand in along the way has to be polished.
- When a submission is mostly AI, I have nothing to give feedback on, because none of it is your own thinking.
- Unsupervised AI prose is "bullshit" in Harry Frankfurt's technical sense (_On Bullshit_): it sounds authoritative, but it doesn't care whether what it says is true.

**What writing actually teaches**

- Clean sentences, organization, and citation are the visible surface. Underneath them is the real work of **gaining authority over a subject**: understanding how the evidence works, who the audience is, and what conversation you're joining.
- **Writing tests whether you understand something.** People think they understand things until they have to explain them. In the "illusion of explanatory depth" studies, people who were sure they knew how a zipper works couldn't explain it on paper . A paper stalls at exactly the points where your understanding runs out.
- **Knowledge transforming, not knowledge telling** . Good writing does more than recite information. It teaches the reader to see the subject the way you see it.
- **Desirable difficulties** . Effortful practice feels less productive in the moment, but you learn more from it.
- Research on AI in learning points the same way. Students with unrestricted AI access can improve in narrow areas but lose a great deal of ability once the AI is taken away Heavy users also remember less of what they "wrote" and judge their own work less accurately.
- "Fake it till you make it" is fine. Trying on an authoritative voice before you've fully earned it is part of learning. Letting AI do it for you isn't.

**Why it matters beyond this class**

- You may never write another research essay. You will keep having to do what writing trains: walk into unfamiliar material, figure out what you don't know, separate strong evidence from weak, anticipate objections, and speak with confidence you've earned.
- This applies in every field: sciences, business, nursing, policy, engineering. Each has its own kinds of evidence and its own ways of talking.
- The job market is rough, and AI is hitting entry-level, routine work hardest (entry-level coding included). Judgment, mastery, and the ability to move into new fields hold up much better. Learning how to learn is the most durable skill you can build.
- AI can help you learn new things if you use it to actually learn them, not to skip them.

**Your thesis statements**

- Almost everyone still has a long way to go, and that's normal. You haven't mastered your material yet.
- Look out for verbs like _show, present, demonstrate_. They usually lead to a claim nobody would dispute ("yes, that is what the text is about"). Aim for something narrower, more specific, and arguable.
- You don't need to rewrite your thesis yet (unless it wasn't really yours to begin with). Start writing and let the claim get sharper as you go.
- Try a "sh*tty first draft" (Anne Lamott): read, take notes, then dump your ideas onto the page without worrying about form. Revise it into a real paper afterward.
