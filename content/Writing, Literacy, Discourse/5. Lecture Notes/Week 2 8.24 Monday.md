The class focused on analyzing Anne Bradstreet's poem and introduced Mary Rowlandson's captivity narrative. ## Analytical Framework Established

- Goal: analyze how language moves an audience, not what a text "means" — a shift away from standardized-test decoding habits. 1
- Key question for any text: What are the lines doing, and how are they doing it? 2
- Applies to both poetry (Bradstreet) and prose rhetoric (Rowlandson). 3

## Bradstreet — Rhetorical & Poetic Devices

Apostrophe & Conceit

- Poem addressed directly to the book = apostrophe (rhetorical/poetic device for addressing an absent person or thing). 4
- Sustained metaphor (conceit): the book as a child — spins off imagery of offspring, kidnapping, clothing, washing, editing. 5
- Linguistic ingenuity comes from fully extending a metaphor; also common in political rhetoric (e.g., "ship of state"). 6

Meter & Rhythm

- Form: rhyming couplets (AABBCC) + largely consistent iambic pentameter (unstressed-stressed, 5 beats/line). 7
- Iambic pentameter is the most natural rhythmic form in English; chosen here as the most conventional, regular structure. 8
- Zane's observation: regular form may reflect Puritan humility and gendered pressure to write traditionally rather than experimentally. 9

Notable Metrical Variations

- Spondee (till snatched): two stresses slow the line, emphasizing the violence of the book being taken. 10
- Pyrrhic (if for, and for): two unstressed beats create quiet momentum before emotionally weighted words like father and mother. 11
- Trochee (halting): rhythm itself inverts on the word "halting" — the line literally halts. 11
- Line "Yet still thou run'st more hobbling than is meet": pyrrhic cluster makes the line trip over itself at the word hobbling — rhythmic imperfection mirrors the meaning. 12
- Pun: "I stretch thy joints to make thee even feet" — "feet" = metrical feet + child's limbs. 13

## Mary Rowlandson — Context & Text

Historical Background

- Rowlandson was an English colonist who survived King Philip's War (Metacomet's War), the deadliest per-capita war in American history: ~5–10% of Massachusetts Bay colonists killed; ~40–50% of engaged Indigenous people killed. 14
- War ended in genocide of Indigenous people; Rowlandson was taken captive and later ransomed. 15

Political Purpose of the Text

- Puritan leaders commissioned the narrative to reframe the war as a divine test of God's chosen people, not a failure of their colonial mission. 16
- Published under male oversight — "friends" publish the woman's narrative, reflecting patriarchal control over women's writing. 17
- Many readers argue Rowlandson's text escapes its directive — personal grief and ambiguity surface beyond the intended religious message. 18

Rhetorical Choices in the Opening

- Events listed without cause-and-effect connectors: this, this, this — no "then"; only one "thus" summarizes the scene. 19
- Agents of violence frequently omitted as grammatical subjects — people are "knocked on the head" without named actors; worth examining why. 20
- Text organized by "removes" (each forced relocation) — students should consider why this structure was chosen. 19
