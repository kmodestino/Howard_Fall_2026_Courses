## Essay Requirements & Guidelines

- **Nature of Work:** The assignment is an **interpretive essay** requiring analysis of a “text,” defined broadly as something that can be closely read, such as a book, film, or poem.
    
- **Constraints:** The essay should not be a social science essay or simply an opinion piece.
    
- **Subject Matter:** Students may choose texts from outside the course. Choosing a new topic rather than revisiting a high school essay is recommended to avoid falling back into old writing habits.
    
- **Thesis Development:** Students should develop three initial ideas to test with classmates. The emphasis is on elaborating and developing those ideas rather than immediately producing a polished final thesis. 1
    

## Rhetorical Analysis: William Apess

The class analyzed the writing of **William Apess**, a Pequot Native American and Methodist preacher writing in the 1830s, focusing on four argumentative “moves”: 1

- **Establishing a Warrant:** Begin with a premise the reader already accepts, such as a shared Christian faith, and use that common ground to make later claims more persuasive.
    
- **Explanation as Argument:** Explain the causes behind a situation—such as systemic reasons for poverty on reservations—as a way of building a broader argument.
    
- **Flipping an Opponent’s Premises:** Use an opponent’s own stated beliefs or words to expose inconsistencies in the opponent’s position.
    
- **Citing Authoritative Documents:** Ground an argument in sources the intended audience considers authoritative, such as the Bible or the Constitution.
