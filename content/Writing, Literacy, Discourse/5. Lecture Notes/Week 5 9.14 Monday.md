## Key Outcomes

The session focused on shifting students from basic high school writing forms, such as the five-paragraph essay, to advanced college-level argumentative writing 1. Professor Modestino emphasized the importance of creating adjudicable, interesting, and developed arguments rather than safe, rubric-driven responses 23.

## College Writing vs. Secondary Education

- Limitations of Five-Paragraph Essays: These forms are often paratactic or cumulative, meaning paragraphs can stand alone without a logical build, making them less convincing to readers who do not already agree with the author 45.
- Rubric-Driven Writing: Standardized tests often reward the presence of specific features (claim, evidence, commentary) rather than the quality of reasoning or the insightfulness of the argument 6.
- Advanced Argumentation: College writing requires subordination, where each paragraph develops the previous one, building toward a conclusion that opens up new implications rather than simply restating the introduction 27.

## Principles of a Strong Argument

- Arguability: A strong thesis must be interesting and contestable, meaning a reasonable person could hold the opposite view and support it with evidence 89.
- Adjudicability: An argument must be capable of being proven or refuted based on evidence; purely subjective judgments (e.g., "this text is moving") are not considered academic arguments 310.
- Scope: Effective claims are specific enough to be developed with depth over a few pages 11.

## Case Study: David Walker's Appeal

- Textual Analysis: The class analyzed David Walker's abolitionist pamphlet to distinguish between factual claims and actual arguments 912.
- Example of a Valid Argument: The claim that Walker grounds his authority in personal experience to reach an audience not swayed by scholarly credentials was identified as a strong basis for an essay because it is adjudicable and allows for counter-arguments 311.
