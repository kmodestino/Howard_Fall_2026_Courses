## The Starting Point: An Intuition

- I started with an intuition about Emerson: that he isn't really a libertarian, even though libertarians often claim him.
- An intuition is a fine place to start, but you can't stay there. The first job is to **test it against the strongest evidence against it**.

---

## Evidence Against My Intuition: Emerson as Proto-Libertarian

- **"Insist on yourself; never imitate."** A strongly freedom- and individualism-focused line. It can sound like creativity, but it can also be read as negating family and community structures.
- **"Ask nothing of men, and, in the endless mutation, thou only firm column must presently appear the upholder of all that surrounds thee."** Ask nothing of others: that's pretty libertarian.
- **The "sturdy lad from New Hampshire or Vermont"** who "teams it, farms it, peddles, keeps a school, preaches, edits a newspaper, goes to Congress, buys a township," and "always, like a cat, falls on his feet," and is "worth a hundred of these city dolls."
    - Like much libertarian thought, this reflects an **agrarian, rural imagination**: country people are imagined as more independent and self-sufficient than people in crowded cities.
- **His dismissal of philanthropy:** "Are they my poor? I tell thee, thou foolish philanthropist, that I grudge the dollar, the dime, the cent, I give to such men as do not belong to me and to whom I do not belong." He goes on to call the dollar he gives to relief societies "a wicked dollar, which by and by I shall have the manhood to withhold."
    - Here we see a **refusal of obligation to strangers**, a refusal of sympathy, something "unmanly" about mutual dependence, and a **masculinist language** that often shows up in the libertarian tradition.
- Taken together, this is strong evidence that Emerson is at least something of a **proto-libertarian**. Facing it honestly means being willing to say my intuition might have been special pleading: defending a writer I like against an ideology I disagree with.

---

## Evidence For My Intuition: Where Emerson Doesn't Fit

- **"The reliance on Property, including the reliance on governments which protect it, is the want of self-reliance."**
    - Even in libertarian theory, the minimal job of government is to protect property rights. Modern libertarianism often treats property as the _basis_ of self-reliance. Emerson says relying on property is the _absence_ of self-reliance.
- People who **"measure their esteem of each other by what each has, and not by what each is"** aren't self-reliant. This reads as a protest against the materialism in American culture that gives rise to libertarianism.
- **"We lie in the lap of immense intelligence, which makes us receivers of its truth and organs of its activity. When we discern justice, when we discern truth, we do nothing of ourselves, but allow a passage to its beams."**
    - The truth of the self-reliant self doesn't come from the individual self alone, but from something **transcendent**: outside and above it.
- **"Who is the Trustee? What is the aboriginal Self, on which a universal reliance may be grounded?"**
    - For the libertarian, the self grounds trust in itself, purely individually. Emerson is asking about a self that comes _before_ all individual selves.
- **Context helps:** Emerson is a romantic and literally a **transcendentalist**. For him, the self isn't a political unit or an economic actor. It's a deeper self present in everyone, a **conduit for something greater**: divine spirit, reason, truth.

---

## Building the Argument

Once you have evidence on both sides, you have to figure out what your argument actually is. I tried three versions:

1. **"Emerson isn't really a libertarian."**
    - This doesn't work. It's just a denial of the common reading, and it ignores all the counter-evidence. Anyone could reply, "What about all these passages that sound libertarian?"
    - Whether he "is" or "isn't" a libertarian is also somewhat beside the point, since libertarianism didn't define itself on its own terms until well after Emerson.
    - In **They Say / I Say** terms: if "they say" Emerson is a proto-libertarian, it isn't enough for me to simply say "no, he isn't."
2. **"Like libertarians, Emerson values the individual, but unlike them, he doesn't value wealth."**
    - True, and closer, but it doesn't _explain_ anything. Why does he hold that position? How does he navigate it? It mostly restates what Emerson already says.
3. **A version that works:**
    - "Self-Reliance" **shares the bootstraps narrative's suspicion of social obligations**, but it **grounds self-reliance in a self that is neither the author nor the owner of its own powers**.
    - On the essay's own terms, what a bootstraps narrative counts as success — **secured property, a fixed social or economic position, the self as a finished product (the "self-made man")** — is a **symptom of failed self-reliance**.
    - This argument doesn't claim Emerson is or isn't a libertarian. It builds a response to libertarianism _out of_ Emerson, and it makes a claim about how we might interpret his understanding of material wealth. Now it's **advancing something**.

---

## A Good Argument Opens Questions

- A productive argument raises new questions. An essay is usually not the end of thinking about a text; it's often the beginning of the research. Questions this reading opens up:
    - What separates **individualism** (the self as owner) from the **individuality** Emerson celebrates (the self as a channel for something transcendent)?
    - What would a **politics** built on that transcendent individuality look like? Libertarianism, or something else?
    - If the self doesn't own its powers, does it ever own **what those powers produce**, like its writing? (Think of Emerson as an author.)
    - If Emerson would have a real critique of libertarianism as it exists today, **why do libertarians still read him so positively and usefully?** Part of the answer may be that Emerson writes **highly quotable lines** that can be extracted from his larger arguments, and some of those lines, taken alone, really do support libertarianism.
- In a five-page essay, you can leave questions like these open. In a book, you'd have to keep going.

---

## Takeaways on Process

- You don't start out knowing what you'll argue. You might start with an **intuition**, but you have to **test it against the text**: gather evidence for it, challenge it, and then build an argument the evidence actually supports and that's **legible to others**.
- "Bias" here doesn't mean social bias; it means something like simply liking or disliking a writer. Everyone comes to a text with anticipations, and reading is the process of confirming or disconfirming them. Starting impressionistically is normal; what matters is subjecting that impression to the rigor of evidence.
- "Yes, Emerson is a libertarian" is a boring essay; people have said it many times. An essay that finesses the question and brings something new to light is doing real work.
- To be clear: this was a **hypothetical exercise**, not an original reading. The reading we arrived at is a common one that many people have made in books and writing on Emerson. The point was to reconstruct _a_ process (not _the_ process) of moving from intuition to evidence to an arguable, interpretive claim.
