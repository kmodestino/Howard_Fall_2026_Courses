## Key Outcomes

The session introduced course logistics, grading policies, and AI use guidelines before pivoting to a close reading of Anne Bradstreet's "The Author to Her Book." Students analyzed the poem's extended child/book metaphor, its visceral emotional tone, and the gendered political stakes of a Puritan woman being published without consent. The instructor contextualized close reading's history — from medieval biblical hermeneutics to the racially fraught New Critics — emphasizing that context and politics are essential, not forbidden, in textual analysis.

## Close Reading: Historical Context

- Named as a university pedagogy in the mid-20th century, primarily for teaching poetry to a growing post-WWII student population. 8
- Roots trace to medieval Catholic biblical hermeneutics (exegesis), then Protestant sola scriptura practices that moved close reading into the home. 91011
- New Critics institutionalized close reading but imposed strict limits — no historical context, no authorial intent, text alone — and were largely pro-segregationist Southerners. 1213
- Counter-tradition: Black critics like James Baldwin and Toni Morrison rejected those limits, insisting on politics and community context while still attending closely to language — a lineage that often goes unacknowledged. 14
- Current approach: Five-step framework (noticing → local claiming → regional observation → global theorizing → scene setting); the first and last steps explicitly move beyond the text. 15

## Anne Bradstreet — "The Author to Her Book"

- Context: Bradstreet lived in Puritan Massachusetts Bay Colony, a strict patriarchal theocracy; her poetry was published without her knowledge by friends, and this poem was appended to the second edition as her response. 1617
- Central conceit: The book is an "ill-formed offspring" — a child sent into the world by its mother — sustained throughout the poem. 18
- Student observations:
    
    - Language is viscerally emotional ("the visage was so irksome in my sight"), conveying genuine distress. 19
    - Friends described as "less wise than true" — unwise to publish without consent, yet loyal in intent — reflects Bradstreet's ambivalence. 20
    - "If for thy father asked, say thou hast none" directly confronts the assumption that only men write, foregrounding gender politics. 21
    - "Rubbing off a spot still made a flaw" raises questions of creator ownership and responsibility, drawing comparisons to Frankenstein and AI creation. 2223

## Political Stakes of the Poem

- Women in Puritan New England could write within strict constraints: perform humility, avoid theology, confine topics to private/devotional matters. 2425
- Anne Hutchinson precedent: Expelled from Massachusetts Bay Colony for leading unauthorized Bible interpretation sessions; later killed in a Native American raid on the frontier — expulsion was life-threatening. 2526
- Hidden dangers in Bradstreet's poem:
    
    - A child without a father = a bastard child, evoking infidelity — dangerous in Puritan society. 27
    - The "lame child" metaphor invokes the gospel figure of the lame beggar healed by Christ — potentially treading into forbidden theological territory. 2728
- Open question: Is the poem a sincere expression of ambivalence, or a strategic performance of gendered humility to avoid Hutchinson's fate? 2627
