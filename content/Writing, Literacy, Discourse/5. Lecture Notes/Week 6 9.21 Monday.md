### Key Outcomes

Prof. Modestino provided guidance on the ethical use of AI in writing, reviewed common mistakes in identifying warrants and evidence from Week 4 assignments, and introduced the requirements for the Week 5 interpretive thesis assignment.

### AI Usage Policy

- **Educational Risk**: AI is discouraged for students learning writing because it works best for those who already possess the skill; relying on it prevents the development of necessary writing abilities.
- **Reliability Issues**: High-end models still have substantial fail rates and compound mistakes, requiring the user to read carefully to identify errors.
- **Learning Process**: Writing requires the freedom to struggle and make mistakes to improve, which AI shortcuts eliminate.

### Feedback on Week 4 Assignments

- **Evidence**: Students struggled with providing substantive evidence; evidence must be precise data or concrete logical steps rather than general gestures.
- **Warrants**: Many students incorrectly tried to underline warrants in texts; warrants are often unstated assumptions that connect evidence to a claim.
- **Example Analysis**: Using a student's example on electric scooters, Prof. Modestino demonstrated that while speed data is evidence, the explanation of why that speed creates a hazard is the warrant.

### Week 5 Assignment: Interpretive Thesis

- **Objective**: Students must propose three possible candidates for an interpretive thesis based on a named object of study.
- **Interpretive vs. Factual**: A valid interpretive question must be contestable; if the answer is a simple "yes/no" or can be found by looking up a fact, it is not interpretive.
- **Scope and Evidence**:
    - **Admissible Evidence**: Includes novels, essays, speeches, and non-print media like films or songs, provided they are analyzed with detailed attention.
    - **Methodology**: Students should use "thick description," focusing on specific, powerful examples to make a larger claim rather than broad surveys.
- **Out-of-Bounds Topics**: Personal narratives (as the primary essay form), pro-con papers on current controversies, and claims requiring wide-population survey data are not permitted.
