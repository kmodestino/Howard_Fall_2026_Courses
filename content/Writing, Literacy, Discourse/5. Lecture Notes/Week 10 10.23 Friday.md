# Five Questions to Answer Before Submitting Your Draft

This is not your final draft. It doesn't need to be polished or fully worked out, but it does need to give you something to work with in revision.

Before you submit you should be able to answer the following questions. You do not need to write these down, but it's what we will discuss in our one-on-one meeting next week.

## Questions

1. **What is your paper arguing or trying to figure out?** You should be able to say it in a sentence or two, even if it's tentative and might change.
2. **What text or passages is your argument built on?** Name the specific moments you're working with, not just the work as a whole.
3. **Where do you interpret your evidence rather than summarize it?** There should be at least a couple of places where you go beyond restating what a quotation says and make a claim about what it means, how it works, or why it matters.
4. **Why does each section come where it does?** You don't need a perfect structure, but you should be able to say what each part contributes to the argument.
5. **What part of this draft are you least sure about?** Knowing where the draft is weak is part of having something to work with.

## Example for Question 3: Summary vs. Interpretation

> "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines." (Emerson, "Self-Reliance")

- **Summary claim:** Emerson says people shouldn't worry about being consistent.
- **Interpretive claim:** Emerson's repeated "little" suggests that his real concern is not honesty but greatness, as the passage's next turn confirms: "To be great is to be misunderstood." On this reading, self-reliance is less a democratic ideal open to everyone than a standard for exceptional individuals, defined against the "little minds" of ordinary people.

The summary restates what the sentence says. The interpretation makes a claim a reasonable reader could dispute (for example, by arguing that Emerson believes every person is capable of greatness), so the writer has to defend it with evidence from the text.
