## Key Outcomes

Students learned to distinguish between cumulative and subordinating paragraphs, focusing on how the latter builds a deep, logically dependent argument 1. The class also analyzed Thomas Paine's "Common Sense" to identify different types of evidence and the role of the warrant—the unstated assumption that must be true for a claim to be valid 23.

## Subordinating vs. Cumulative Paragraphs

- Cumulative Paragraphs: Consist of a topic sentence followed by details that build on the initial idea but could logically be reordered without losing meaning 4.
- Subordinating Paragraphs: Each subsequent sentence is logically dependent on the one preceding it, creating a deeper argument where the order of sentences is essential 1.
- Expansion Methods: Points can be expanded through restatement (new language/imagery), evidence, mechanism (describing how something works), or narrowing (refining the point to a specific case) 56.

## Framework for Analyzing Claims

Every claim consists of three essential components 2:

- Evidence: The data or logic used to support the claim (e.g., deductive reasoning from a first principle) 7.
- Claim: The overall point being supported (e.g., that hereditary succession is evil) 2.
- Warrant: The underlying assumption shared by the writer and audience that makes the evidence relevant to the claim 3.

## Types of Evidence

- Deductive Reasoning: Starting with a general principle and drawing a specific logical conclusion 7.
- Inductive Reasoning: Observing specific examples to derive a general principle 8.
- Empirical Evidence: Data, statistics, or historical facts 89.
- Anecdotal/Testimony: Personal experiences; while often insufficient for science, they are legitimate in specific contexts.
- Scriptural/Textual: Using religious or literary texts as a form of wisdom or historical proof 10.
- Analogy: Drawing lessons from a similar situation to apply to the current one 11.
