## Upcoming assignment: one full body paragraph (due next Thursday at midnight)
- Draft one body paragraph from your own essay, built around substantial evidence and analysis, with quotations cited in MLA format.
- If you haven't yet picked the specific objects you're analyzing (films, shows, speeches, books, essays, ads, etc.), you need to do it now.
- The paragraph makes a *claim about the specific evidence in front of you*. That claim is one piece of your larger argument. Don't just repeat your thesis.
- The goal is a paragraph where the quotations and your thinking about them do most of the work, not summary or general statements.
- **Integrating quotations:**
  - Bring quoted words into a sentence of your own. Don't drop them in on their own.
  - Use a signal phrase ("Douglas writes that…") or weave short phrases into your own grammar.
  - Avoid big block quotes. Quote the specific details your reader needs to see.
- **Analysis:**
  - Explain what the quoted language shows and why it supports your claim.
  - Good analysis looks closely at particular words, images, or phrasing rather than restating the quote in other words.
- **Required structure (deliberately rigid so you can practice the core skill):**
  1. Your current working thesis at the top of the page. It's fine if it's still in progress.
  2. An opening sentence stating one claim that supports the thesis.
  3. At least two integrated quotations, each with an MLA in-text citation.
  4. At least two sentences of analysis after each quotation, connecting it back to the claim.
  5. A closing sentence connecting the paragraph's claim back to the thesis.
  6. A Works Cited entry below the paragraph for every source cited.
- When you revise this paragraph for your actual draft, you're free to loosen the structure and change it up rhetorically.
- The paragraph can come from anywhere in your essay. It doesn't matter if it depends on earlier context. What matters is that the claim is supported by the evidence you give.
- You probably won't write it in order. You'll find the important quotes first, work out what claim they support, then figure out how that fits the whole essay. That process is also a good way to refine a thesis you're struggling to narrow down, because detailed analysis often comes before knowing what your bigger argument is.

## Why citation exists
- The core purpose of a citation is so your reader can find what you're quoting, confirm it exists, and read it in its original context. Giving credit is secondary, since you can credit someone just by naming them.
- A hyperlink to an original post or article works as an online citation system. A written paper needs a formal way to describe the source accurately.
- Different disciplines use different systems: MLA, Chicago, APA. MLA is designed to be relatively unobtrusive on the page.
  - APA and Chicago put more apparatus on the page because those fields need it.
  - Literary analysis can just point the reader to the Works Cited page.

## How MLA works
- **In-text citation:** at the end of the sentence, in parentheses, give the author's name and the page number.
  - If you've already named the author in the sentence (and use only one source by them), give just the page.
  - The period goes after the parentheses. No comma before the parentheses, and no punctuation inside the quotation marks.
  - For online texts without page numbers, use the finest locating unit available:
    - a chapter number, e.g. (Chapter 10)
    - a paragraph number, but only if the paragraphs are few enough to count reasonably
- **Works Cited entry:** a full listing on a separate page, which your in-text citation points to.
  - Last name, first name, then the title in italics, then the original publication year.
  - Then where your copy comes from: publisher and edition year for a book, or the site and a short URL for an online source. Don't paste extremely long URLs.
- Perfection isn't the standard. The citation just has to work.

## Citations and AI tools
- Automating citations is normal scholarly practice; citation managers have existed for decades.
- But AI chatbots are notoriously bad at citations and links, and free tools are especially bad.
  - These models predict plain language, and citations and URLs don't look like plain language.
  - They also tend to pull in a citation from elsewhere in the conversation and put it in the wrong place.
- Asking a tool to format a source you give it into MLA is probably fine. Asking it to generate citations from scratch is likely to produce errors.
- If you never learn the format yourself, you won't be able to spot those errors. It's often less work to just do it yourself.

## Discussion: how Douglas uses evidence (the ships on the Chesapeake Bay)
- Douglas is telling a story rather than writing an analysis, but his stories carry arguments.
- **The small claim:** the ships' white sails that delight free people appear to him as "shrouded ghosts" that torment him with thoughts of his condition. Enslavement changes what he sees.
- **The evidence:** an extended apostrophe (an address to an absent person, thing, or concept) to the ships, framed as spoken before God.
  - Douglas names the device himself while calling it his "rude way." That's a striking juxtaposition from a self-taught, deeply read rhetorician.
- **The key question: what's the warrant?** This is a reconstructed, imagined speech. He couldn't know his exact earlier thoughts word for word. In a social science paper, a made-up quote like that wouldn't be admissible evidence. So why does it work here?
- **Points raised in discussion:**
  - The line "all boys are bound to someone" works as a kind of counterpoint: not everyone is bound the way he is.
  - Ships carry a literal history for an enslaved person: the slave trade, being shipped and sold. They mean captivity, not adventure or escape.
  - The speech reads like a theatrical aside, emotionally charged and "from the heart," voicing both the wonders of freedom and the pain of lacking it. The warrant is partly that **Douglas has authority over his own experience.** For an enslaved person writing for the first time, that is a contested claim to make, not a self-evident one.
  - The vivid, action-filled details ("Could I but swim! If I could fly!") make a subjective experience felt by readers who never lived it. Rhetoric can carry a claim by making it vividly experiential.
  - He inverts a common theme in American literature by white men, where ships and the sea stand for freedom and adventure.
  - Addressing the apostrophe to God puts his feeling on ground his Christian Northern readers share. This is part of his broader skill at taking up the modes of address his audience values most and turning them back to expose the nation's hypocrisy. "What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?" does this on a larger scale.
- **The passage's turns:**
  - It moves from suffering, to resolve ("I will run away… get caught, or get clear"), then back to resignation ("I am not the only slave in the world… I am but a boy").
  - He describes himself as "goaded almost to madness" one moment and reconciling himself to his "wretched lot" the next.
  - The escape here is still fantasy. The ships are ghosts both of the history of enslavement and of a desired future he doesn't yet know how to bring about.
  - The passage lifts the reader toward freedom and then pulls back down. That supports the original claim and fits the larger narrative arc: he isn't free yet.
- **Looking ahead:** the fight with Covey offers a different kind of evidence. It isn't a quotation but a thick description of an event that stands as evidence for a stage in his journey. The same question applies: where does Douglas's account get its authority to count as evidence?
- **Bigger context:**
  - Slave narratives often opened with prefaces by white abolitionists vouching for their truth, as if Black testimony couldn't stand on its own.
  - This echoes a longer U.S. history of Black testimony being excluded from courts, juries, and public discourse.
  - Douglas is building the terms under which his own experience counts as evidence. You can already see him straining against the authorizing preface by William Lloyd Garrison.
  - He would later leave Garrison behind, found his own newspaper, and write under his own authority.
