# Week 2 — Sentence Imitation: Parataxis and Hypotaxis

**Due:** two labeled examples from this week's reading, plus three original sentences written twice each — once paratactically and once hypotactically — and a short reflection. No more than one page.

## What this means

Coordination and subordination are the two basic ways English marks the relationship between clauses. This exercise asks you to feel the difference by writing the same content both ways.

**Parataxis** places clauses side by side as equals. Each clause could stand alone as a complete sentence; they're joined by coordinating conjunctions (_and, but, or, so, yet, for_) or simply set beside one another with a comma or semicolon. Parataxis doesn't tell you how the clauses relate — it just adds them, one after another, and leaves you to infer the connection. It's the syntax of the King James Bible ("And the earth was without form, and void; and darkness was upon the face of the deep"). Rowlandson's narrative opens in this mode:

> "On the tenth of February 1675, came the Indians with great numbers upon Lancaster: their first coming was about sunrising; hearing the noise of some guns, we looked out; several houses were burning, and the smoke ascending to heaven."

Four clauses, none ranked above another, separated by a colon and semicolons that assert no logic at all. The sentence registers what happened in the order it was perceived. It offers no explanation, and no interpretation.

**Hypotaxis** ranks its clauses. One clause is grammatically independent; the rest are dependent, introduced by a subordinating conjunction (_because, although, since, while, if, when, that_) or a relative pronoun (_who, which, that_) that states outright how the parts relate — as cause, purpose, concession, condition, or time. Hypotaxis makes the logic explicit instead of implied. Rowlandson turns to this mode in the Second Remove, when she stops reporting events and begins interpreting them:

> "But the Lord renewed my strength still, and carried me along, that I might see more of His power; yea, so much that I could never have thought of, had I not experienced it."

The subordinating words do the interpretive work here: _that I might see_ makes her survival purposive rather than accidental, _so much that_ measures the result against a cause, and the inverted conditional _had I not experienced it_ marks the whole claim as knowledge available only through suffering. This is worth noticing as a pattern. Where Rowlandson reports, she tends toward parataxis; where she reads events as providence, she reaches for subordination. The syntax is doing theological work.

**What changes when you recast:** here is that same sentence made paratactic —

> The Lord renewed my strength, and He carried me along, and I saw more of His power, and I could never have imagined so much.

The events are identical. What disappears is the frame. The paratactic version leaves you to decide whether God strengthened her _in order that_ she might see His power, or whether she simply saw it afterward — the very question the hypotactic original settles.

## Instructions

1. From this week's reading (the opening and the First and Second Removes), find one sentence that is clearly **paratactic** and one that is clearly **hypotactic**. Copy each out and label it. Underline the coordinating or subordinating words that make it one or the other.
2. Pick a single idea, image, or event — any topic you like — and write it twice:
    - as a **paratactic** sentence (coordinated or juxtaposed independent clauses; no subordinating conjunctions)
    - as a **hypotactic** sentence (a main clause with at least one subordinate clause stating a logical relation — cause, purpose, concession, condition, or time)Do this for three different topics (six sentences total).
3. In two or three sentences, describe what changed between your paratactic and hypotactic versions: what stayed implicit, and what got made explicit?

**Submit as a numbered list:** your two labeled examples from the reading, your three paired sentences (six total, each marked paratactic or hypotactic), and your closing reflection. No more than one page.

 Week 3 — Subordinating Paragraphs

**Due:** Monday Sept. 7th at Midnight, double spaced 12 pt. font.

**Two items** — (1) a subordinate paragraph you find elsewhere (in this sermon, another assigned reading, or something else you're reading), labeled by level (L1, L2, etc.) with a brief note on what move each level makes on the one before it; and (2) a paragraph of your own — a topic sentence (Level 1) followed by three levels of subordinate sentences (Levels 2–4), the whole reading as one coherent paragraph, not four separate sentences.

**What this means:**

This exercise builds a paragraph the way the sentence exercise built a sentence, one level of specificity at a time. But the numbering isn't just sequence — it's a claim about logical relationship. Each level doesn't just come after the one above it; it is generated by it, in one of a few ways: it can restate the prior claim more concretely, supply the mechanism or evidence for it, or narrow its scope. If you can delete a sentence and the paragraph still makes the same point with the same force, that sentence wasn't really subordinate — it was just added.

- **Level 1** is your topic sentence — the most general statement, the claim the whole paragraph exists to support.
- **Level 2** develops Level 1 directly — it should answer "how so?" or "in what way?" about L1, not introduce a new but related idea.
- **Level 3** develops Level 2 the same way — it earns its place by explaining or particularizing L2, not by returning to L1.
- **Level 4** develops Level 3 — by this level you should be at the most concrete, specific point the paragraph reaches.

A useful test: read only L1 and L4 together. If L4 sounds like a wild non sequitur next to L1, the chain in between is doing its job — it's the only thing making that jump legible. If L4 could stand next to L1 with no bridge needed, the levels weren't really subordinating.

**Note on why this matters for argumentative writing:** This is not just a sentence-craft drill. Subordination — each unit justifying, qualifying, or specifying the one above it — is the basic structural logic of an argument, not just of a paragraph. Argumentative prose tends to resist purely _cumulative_ structure (where sentences add loosely related, coordinate details at roughly the same level of generality, the way descriptive or narrative prose often does) because an argument's job is to keep narrowing toward a warranted, specific claim, not to accumulate texture around a general one. Recognizing the difference between "adding" and "subordinating" is, in miniature, the difference between description and argument.

**Example of Cumulative Paragraph:**

- L1 — The city was dying.
- L2 — Its streets, once crowded, stood empty by noon.
- L2 — Shop windows, dusty and dark, reflected no one walking past.
- L2 — Only a stray cat paused there, sniffing at a torn awning before moving on.

Notice this example is cumulative not subordinate — each level adds a coordinate concrete detail rather than logically entailing the one above it. That's fine for description. Watch how the second example differs.

**Example of a subordinating structure (Edwards, "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God"):**

- L1 — "That the reason why they are not fallen already, and don't fall now, is only that God's appointed time is not come."
- L2 — "For it is said that when that due time, or appointed time comes, their foot shall slide."
- L3 — "Then they shall be left to fall, as they are inclined by their own weight."
- L4 — "God won't hold them up in these slippery places any longer, but will let them go; and then, at that very instant, they shall fall to destruction; as he that stands in such slippery declining ground on the edge of a pit that he can't stand alone, when he is let go he immediately falls and is lost."

L2 supplies the scriptural warrant for L1's claim about timing; L3 draws out the practical consequence once that warrant is met; L4 renders the fall itself in the sermon's characteristic image, at the most concrete point the passage reaches.

**Instructions:**

1. Find one more example of a subordinate paragraph from Edwards and label its sentences L1 through L4 (or however many levels it has, it can have more, but each new sentence should represent a new level) briefly noting in a phrase or two what kind of move each level makes on the one before it (restatement, evidence, mechanism, narrowing).
2. Write one topic sentence (Level 1) of your own.
3. Write a Level 2 sentence that develops the topic sentence directly.
4. Write a Level 3 sentence that develops the Level 2 sentence.
5. Write a Level 4 sentence that develops the Level 3 sentence.
6. A description of how each of your sentences develops from the previous one's along the same lines of your analysis of the Edwards paragraph.

---

_Don't just clear the bar. A four-sentence paragraph that technically subordinates correctly but plays it safe teaches you less than one where you push the logic somewhere genuinely difficult to sustain — try a topic sentence abstract enough that getting to L4 without breaking the chain is actually hard. This is a low-stakes place to test how far you can carry a line of reasoning before it snaps; use it that way._

# Week 4 — Claim, Data, Warrant

_Due: one paragraph analyzing a text's claim, data, and warrant, plus one original paragraph of your own with claim, data, and warrant clearly marked._

**What this means:**

Every argument, whether it's a Supreme Court brief or a bar bet, rests on the same three-part structure. Learning to see it will change how you read, and how you write.

- **Claim** — the arguable statement you want your reader to accept. Not a fact (facts aren't arguable), but a position someone could reasonably push back on.
- **Data** — the fact, example, or evidence you offer as support. This is the part most students already do instinctively: "here's my proof."
- **Warrant** — the connective tissue between the two. It's the assumption that has to be true for your data to actually support your claim. This is the part almost everyone skips, because it usually feels too obvious to state out loud, which is exactly why it's worth stating.

Here's the test: if you can insert "_because_" between your data and your claim and the sentence still doesn't make sense without one more idea, that missing idea is your warrant.

**Example:**

- Claim: _"The new library hours will hurt working students."_
- Data: _"The library now closes at 6pm, before most working students get off their evening shifts."_
- Warrant: _"Students who cannot physically get to the library during its open hours cannot use its resources."_

Notice the warrant isn't a new fact, it's the _reasoning move_ that makes the data relevant to the claim at all. Someone could accept the data (library closes at 6pm) and still reject the claim, if they rejected the warrant (maybe they'd argue students can use the library's resources online). That's what makes the warrant the most vulnerable, and most important, part of any argument to identify, whether you're analyzing someone else's or building your own.

**Instructions:**

1. Choose one paragraph from _Common Sense_ or one grievance from the Declaration.
2. In one sentence each, identify its claim, its data, and its warrant.
3. Then write your own original paragraph (any topic) with a clear claim, data, and warrant. Underline each.

### Week 5 — Candidate Theses

**Due:** three candidate thesis claims for your essay, each with one sentence of evidence and one sentence of warrant, plus a closing sentence naming the strongest.

#### The essay this leads to

A five-page interpretive essay on a question of your own choosing, arguing a contestable claim about the meaning of particular historical or cultural evidence. Not a summary, not a report, not a reflection.

#### What counts as a thesis

A thesis claim is the single sentence stating the specific, contestable position your essay will argue. It is not a fact and not a vague preference:

- **Fact:** Walker's _Appeal_ is organized into a Preamble and four Articles.
- **Preference:** Walker's _Appeal_ is a courageous and passionate document.
- **Thesis:** By arranging his pamphlet as a Preamble and four Articles, Walker does more than borrow the Constitution's authority — he stages the _Appeal_ as a rival founding document whose form indicts the original as an unratified promise, binding on no one until the people it excluded are counted among its authors.

The third is arguable — a reader could hold that articles were a conventional way to divide a pamphlet and carry no constitutional claim at all — and it is answerable with evidence you can actually get to.

#### What counts as the right kind of question

Your question must ask **what a text meant, how it worked on the people who read or heard it, or why it took the form it did.** Answering it must require you to interpret particular evidence closely — novels, essays, speeches, sermons, letters, pamphlets, advice manuals, editorials, prefaces, schoolbooks, and the like — rather than to survey opinion, count cases, or recommend a policy. Non-print evidence is admissible if you read it with the same attention you would give a page.

Two-part test. If your question fails either part, revise it.

1. **Meaning test.** Could two careful readers, looking at the same passage, disagree about what it means? If the answer is settled by looking something up, it is a research question for a different course.
2. **Evidence test.** Can you name, right now, three to five specific things you would read closely? Not "sources about" your topic — the actual texts your interpretation will work on.

**Out of bounds for this essay:** personal narrative; a report that informs without arguing; an evaluation of whether a work is good; a pro/con paper on a current controversy; a claim about a population that would need survey data to support. Any of these can be converted into an interpretive question. _Should schools be allowed to ban books?_ is out of bounds. _What idea of the young reader is assumed by both sides of the 1950s campaign against comic books?_ is in bounds, because it asks what a set of readable documents claimed and how they worked on the audience they addressed.

#### Instructions

1. Name your object of study and your evidence base in one or two sentences: what specifically you will be interpreting, and where you can get it.
2. Pose one real question about it — a question, not a topic. "Violence in Walker's _Appeal_" is a topic. "Why does Walker keep turning to address white readers directly in a pamphlet he arranged to have carried south to Black ones?" is a question.
3. Draft **three different** thesis statements that could answer it. Different answers, not three phrasings of one answer.
4. For each, add:
    - **Evidence** (one sentence): the specific passage, document, or detail that would support the claim.
    - **Warrant** (one sentence): the assumption that makes that evidence count as support — state it explicitly, since it is the part readers dispute without saying so.
5. Close with one sentence: which of the three is strongest, and why.

**Worked example of 4:**

> **Claim:** Walker's article structure stages the _Appeal_ as a rival founding document rather than a petition to an existing one.  
> **Evidence:** The title page announces the work in four Articles together with a Preamble, reproducing the sequence of the Constitution's own divisions, and Walker ends by quoting the Declaration's self-evident truths back at white readers as terms they themselves have broken.  
> **Warrant:** When a writer adopts the formal architecture of a document his audience already recognizes, the borrowed form makes an argument of its own, independent of anything the sentences assert.

#### Length and scope

Five pages. A question you cannot answer in five pages is the most common failure at this stage — narrower questions produce better essays, not thinner ones. If your question would require a chapter, cut it down to the single case you can actually argue.

### Week 7 — Quotation Integration

**Due:** one paragraph integrating a quotation with two sentences of analysis.

**What this means:**

Integrating a quotation means working someone else's exact words into your own sentence grammatically — not dropping a quotation in as its own sentence and moving on. Analysis is different from summary: summary restates what a quotation says in different words; analysis explains what it means for your argument — why it matters, what it reveals, how it works.

Weak (summary): "Douglass says he fought back against Covey. This shows he was brave." Stronger (analysis): "When Douglass writes that he 'seized him with both hands by his collar,' the verb seized — sudden, physical, active — marks the exact sentence where the narrative shifts from endurance to resistance."

**Instructions:**

- Choose one quotation (under 25 words) from this week's reading.
- Write one sentence that grammatically works the quotation into your own sentence.
- Write two sentences of analysis — not summary — explaining what the quotation reveals and why it matters.

### Week 9 — Structural Outline

**Due:** a structural outline for your essay.

**What this means:**

A structural outline (or reverse outline) lists one line per paragraph, stating what each paragraph does — its job — rather than what it says — its content. "Introduces the claim," "presents the strongest evidence," "raises and answers the counterargument," "closes by returning to the opening image." This lets you see your essay's shape the way an X-ray shows a skeleton instead of skin.

**Instructions:**

- Write one line per paragraph you plan to include (or, if you've already started drafting, one line per paragraph you've written).
- Each line states the paragraph's function, not its content.
- Aim for 6–10 lines.

### Week 10 — Full Draft

**Due:** a full draft of an argumentative essay: claim, evidence, counterargument, structure.

**What this means:**

A counterargument is a genuine, fairly stated version of the strongest objection to your claim, followed by your response to it — not a weak version you can easily knock down. An essay without one usually falls apart the moment a sharp reader pushes back.

**Instructions:**

- 4–6 pages.
- State your claim early and clearly.
- Develop it with evidence from at least two texts read so far.
- Include one paragraph that states and answers a real counterargument.
- Organize roughly according to your Week 9 outline.

### Week 11 — Audience Revision

**Due:** one paragraph revised twice, for two different readers.

**What this means:**

Audience revision means rewriting the same content with different assumptions about what the reader already believes or knows. The claim and evidence don't change. What changes is word choice, what you explain versus assume, and how directly you state things.

**Instructions:**

- Take one paragraph from your Week 10 draft.
- Rewrite it once as if the reader already disagrees with your claim — anticipate and address their objection more directly.
- Rewrite it again as if the reader has never read the text you're discussing — add context you'd otherwise assume.
- Submit all three versions (original plus two revisions) with one sentence noting what changed in each.

### Week 12 — Editing Passes

**Due:** a revised draft, with two edited passes marked.

**What this means:**

Three editing targets, from Joseph Williams' Style: concision (cutting words that don't earn their place — redundancy, throat-clearing, needless hedging), cohesion (making sentences flow into each other — old information at the start of a sentence, new information at the end), and emphasis (putting the most important idea in the most prominent position, usually the end of the sentence).

**Instructions:**

- Take your Week 10–11 draft.
- Do one full pass focused only on concision — cut at least 10% of your word count without losing meaning.
- Do a second full pass focused on cohesion or emphasis (your choice) — mark or comment on what you changed and why for at least five sentences.

### Week 13 — Peer Edit

**Due:** a marked-up peer draft with a short editorial note.

**What this means:**

A peer edit is not proofreading for typos and it is not "I liked it." It means identifying specific places where the argument, evidence, or structure could be stronger, and explaining why — treating editing as part of the interpretive work, not just correction.

**Instructions:**

- Exchange drafts with a partner.
- Mark at least five specific spots with a concrete suggestion — not "this is unclear" but "this claim needs a counterargument" or "this paragraph could move earlier."
- Write a short paragraph to your partner summarizing the two or three biggest opportunities for revision you saw.
