# **Discussion Commitments**

- **We start from respect, not suspicion.** Everyone here has thought seriously about this material and deserves to be heard that way. Assume good faith before you assume error.
- **The goal isn't the correct answer — it's the best available one.** Interpretation in the humanities doesn't converge on a final truth the way a math proof does. We're reasoning as people situated in history, working from where we stand, toward the strongest account we can build and defend against real alternatives. "Best available, tested against the alternatives" is the standard — not "solved."
- **This is a room for thinking together, not winning.** Disagreement will come up, and that's fine — but it's a tool for getting clearer on an idea, not a contest. If a back-and-forth stops helping anyone understand the text better and starts just being about who's right, that's the signal to let it go.
- **A wrong claim built on real engagement beats a correct claim built on none.** Saying "the sky is blue" is true and worth nothing. An argument that's mistaken but shows you actually wrestled with the text — traced an image, tested a reading, noticed a tension — is doing the real work of this class. That's what we're here for, and it's what grading rewards: analysis and evidence over safe correctness.
- **Say what you actually think.** Don't hedge everything into mush to stay safe. A clear claim that turns out wrong is more useful to the room than a vague one nobody can engage with.
- **Listen like the other person might be right.** Before you push back, make sure you could say their point back to them in a way they'd recognize. If you can't, you're not ready to disagree with it yet.
- **Changing your mind counts as participation.** So does pausing to think instead of answering right away. Neither is a loss
