Injustice is felt before it is reasoned. When we witness something we know to be wrong — something that betrays our sense of how the law should work, of how human beings should treat one another, that upends our sense of rightness and decency — it registers as a shock, an outrage of deep and abiding emotional impact, both before and long after we understand exactly what moral law has been betrayed.

Human cultures across the world have attempted to stage this feeling in drama, and in staging it, to offer us insight and catharsis. Since antiquity, the rituals of theater and performance have been a paramount site for staging the gap between the demands of moral and divine law and the blindness, flaws, and frailty of human beings and human societies. In this class we will explore the long history of tragic drama, from ancient Athens to the works of James Baldwin, as a ritual of witnessing and expressing our sense of justice betrayed by incomplete moral orders.

Beginning with Aeschylus's _Oresteia_, we will witness an allegory of the birth of law and legal systems out of the chaos and violence of cycles of vengeance. We will explore moral dilemma — two conflicting and equally valid obligations — in _Antigone_. Shakespeare's _Hamlet_ offers us a conflicted modern self, uncertain of his obligations after the killing of his father. And modern African American drama brings us forward to the present, dramatizing the effects of unjust legal systems.

Alongside the plays, we will read excerpts of philosophy. Both the aesthetic analysis of drama and theories of justice and moral law will illuminate the texts. Throughout, we will take the emotional response to injustice seriously as something societies must redress — in ritual, in religion, in social transformation — in order to achieve anything resembling a stable and inclusive system of justice. In doing so, we will ask: does drama merely purge the emotions, or does it drive us toward change? Can tragic drama offer complex societies a site to witness and find consensus about injustices that would otherwise remain invisible? What role does the evocation of emotion play in forming the communities that gather around performance?
