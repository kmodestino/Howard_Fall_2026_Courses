## Recap: Classical Texts and the "Pre-Political"

- The _Oresteia_, _Antigone_, and parts of Aristotle imagine a mythical **Bronze Age**: royal houses, honor, eye-for-an-eye violence. In that world the tragedy and chaos call for a political state and its system of justice (e.g., the court at the end of the _Oresteia_).
- **The "pre-political" is a fantasy.** Even Aristotle (_Politics_) defines humans as political animals.
- **Modern versions of the same fantasy** are "paleo" claims about diet, politics, sex, and so on, and much of **evolutionary psychology**. They deduce what "natural" humans must have been like instead of working from evidence.
- **The actual record shows enormous diversity:**
    - Anatomically modern humans: ~300,000 BCE
    - Agriculture: ~10,000 BCE
    - First writing: ~3200 BCE
    - Hundreds of thousands of years of varied social, political, family, and religious arrangements. There was no single "natural" way of being human.
- **These myths are about the writer's own present.**
    - The _Oresteia_ is about Athens and its political crises. Linear B tablets show that Bronze Age states already had bureaucracies and managed compensation for crimes.
    - _Antigone_ can be read as a warning to Athens: dishonoring family claims creates internal strife.
- **Classical justice is always communal.** The plays never question that a person acts within communities (family, city, gods) that have just claims on them. Tragedy comes when those claims conflict. Aristotle's virtue is likewise cultivated within a community, through roles and specific contexts.

## What Is "Modernity"?

- **The modern self:** the person increasingly imagined as standing _outside_ communal contexts, alone with conscience, reason, or God. Justice becomes an inner faculty supposedly felt equally by everyone everywhere.
- Locke is often treated as the philosophical origin of the modern self, and Shakespeare as the artistic one.
- **When did modernity begin?** It depends on your emphasis:
    - **Colonial/racial world order:** 1492, with Columbus and the fall of Granada and expulsion of the Moors (the _Reconquista_), which helped establish a Christian, white European identity and the foundation for global empires
    - **Religion:** the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople (1453), then the Protestant Reformation (Luther, 1517)
    - **Politics:** the Treaty of Westphalia (1648): national sovereignty, the absolutist state, religious pluralism. Or the American and French revolutions.
    - **Economics:** mercantilism and the rising bourgeoisie, or the Industrial Revolution and capitalism, settler colonies, the Atlantic slave trade
    - **Art/philosophy:** Renaissance perspective, Machiavelli's _The Prince_
- **Shakespeare** lived in the late English Renaissance, after the English Reformation, during the founding of Jamestown. He was a "self-made" author who earned his living from the theater and sold books under his own name.
- **Locke**, writing nearly a century later, lived through the English Civil War, the Restoration, and the Glorious Revolution. He drafted the constitution of the Carolina colony and laid the groundwork for the English Enlightenment and the American Revolution.

## Locke's Reputation

- He is popularly celebrated as an influence on the American founders (Jefferson).
- Among scholars he is one of the most criticized philosophers, attacked from the left, right, and center. He is often invoked to explain modern individualism, thin or materialistic ideas of the mind, and politics focused on property and contracts rather than social well-being.

### Charles Taylor's Critique: The "Punctual Self"

- Locke pictures the self as a **point of pure awareness** standing apart from feelings, habits, upbringing, and beliefs. It can step back, examine them, and decide what to keep.
    - E.g., "I'll drop a belief if I can't rationally justify it." _Who_ is the self doing the stepping back?
    - In the classical world, you didn't choose your religious beliefs. You _were_ them.
- Locke ties this to **freedom**: we can pause, deliberate, and not simply do what we're told. Identity rests on what you can remember and take responsibility for, so the modern self is built to be held accountable.
- **Taylor's objection:** we become selves by caring about some things more than others, by living among people, and by understanding our lives as stories, not by impartially weighing everything.
    - We never really step outside our values. Locke's own prizing of freedom is itself a strong value, not a neutral conclusion.
- Taylor doesn't throw the ideal out: thinking for yourself is a real achievement. The problem is pretending we do it from pure reason, outside our lives. That makes it hard to say what we actually value.
- Locke himself was a sincere Christian who saw reason as God's gift. Even so, his ideas became foundational for 18th-century British deism and atheism.

## Locke's _Second Treatise_: The State of Nature

- **The law of nature is reason** (Ch. 2, §6): "being all equal and independent, no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions."
    - Compare the Declaration of Independence: "self-evident" truths; "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness."
- **Consent of the governed:**
    - For Locke, consent is essentially _hypothetical_: we consented at some distant point.
    - For Jefferson, consent can be _withdrawn_ through revolution.
    - Locke allows dissolving a government only after "a long train of abuses." Has any population ever actually consented, with a real option to say no?
- **Responses to wrongdoing in the state of nature:**
    1. **Reparation:** only the injured party can seek it (if someone takes your horse, _you_ can take it back).
    2. **Punishment:** _anyone_ can punish the wrongdoer, who has become "dangerous to mankind." The **executive power** (to punish, even to kill) belongs to everyone.
- **State of war:** someone who tries to kill or enslave you has entered a state of war, and you may resist until the aggressor offers peace and repair.
- **The problem is passion.** People judging their own cases "go too far," refuse restitution, and retaliate out of proportion. Wrongs multiply into endless violence, a kind of fall from a rational Eden.
- **Government as the solution:** the state takes a **monopoly on executive power** (criminal law) and on deciding harms between parties (civil law: lawsuits, contracts, torts). This parallels the _Oresteia_'s move from vendetta to courts.

### The Key Difference from the Classical Myths

- Classical selves answer for their own honor, family, or gods. **Locke's self is responsible for justice on behalf of all humanity.**
- After government exists, the self keeps the burden of **continually judging** whether the whole system accords with reason. Yet it is considered too passionate and partial to **act**.
- **The modern bind:** forever able to judge, forever constrained from acting. We can see injustice everywhere but seem to have little power to change it.
- **Impartiality as the standard of justice:** passion, partiality, and attachment to community, religion, family, and tradition count as distortions. Yet these are often exactly why we feel the call of justice at all. Locke's vision is one of **self-alienation**.
- **A common critique:** the "impartial" self is really the **white male property owner**, someone insulated from the world by property. Locke's draft constitution of the Carolinas wrote **slavery and settler land theft** into law, because property was paramount.

### Historical Context (from discussion)

- The **Glorious Revolution** (1688) established a constitutional monarchy bound by Parliament and law. It also brought a limited **religious liberty**: freedom among Protestant denominations after a century of conflict.
- Many American revolutionaries saw themselves as claiming the Glorious Revolution's rights, which Britain denied its colonies. That is partly why they turned to Locke.

## Looking Ahead: _Hamlet_

- _Hamlet_ is a **tragedy of the modern self**. Hamlet can see evil, judge it, and devise plots to expose it, but can't find grounds to carry out justice.
- Hamlet does act, often in unjust ways, but never follows through on the central problem of justice he faces.
- Shakespeare wrote before Locke, but already explored the binds created by a self that is supposed to judge everything rationally.
