## _Antigone_: Plot Review

- **Background:** After Oedipus's fall, his sons **Polynices** (older) and **Eteocles** (younger) agree to alternate rule of Thebes. Eteocles refuses to hand power back.
- Polynices raises an army from **Argos** and attacks Thebes. The brothers kill each other in single combat.
- **Creon** (brother of Jocasta) takes the throne and decrees that Eteocles gets a hero's burial. Polynices, a traitor, is to be left unburied, and anyone who buries him dies.
- For Greeks this is serious: burial was a duty owed by family to the dead, and an unburied body offended the gods of the underworld.
- **Antigone** asks **Ismene** to help bury Polynices. Ismene refuses ("we're just women"). Antigone covers the body with dust, returns after it's uncovered, and is caught.
- Antigone tells Creon his decree can't override the **unwritten laws of the gods**. Creon, insecure in a new rule after a civil war, condemns her to be **sealed alive in a tomb**. She rejects Ismene's attempt to share the blame.
- **Haemon** (Creon's son, engaged to Antigone) reports that the city sympathizes with her. Creon accuses him of siding with her out of love rather than justice.
- **Tiresias** warns that the gods are offended: Creon keeps the dead above ground and puts the living below.
- Creon relents too late. Antigone has hanged herself. Haemon tries to kill his father and then kills himself. **Eurydice** (Creon's wife) kills herself, cursing Creon. Creon is left alone and broken.
- **Core conflict:** Two claims, each presented as absolute. Antigone speaks for family and the gods of the dead; Creon speaks for the law of the city and the punishment of treason. Neither gives ground until the cost is total.

## Why Hegel (and Why He's Hard)

- **Enormously influential:** existentialism, Simone de Beauvoir, the Frankfurt School and critical theory, Marxist cultural theory, anti-colonial thinkers like **Frantz Fanon** and **Aimé Césaire**, **Martin Luther King Jr.**, Gadamer, Charles Taylor, Slavoj Žižek.
- **Hard to read** because he wrote at the end of the German Idealist tradition, which had developed a highly specialized vocabulary.
- **His major insight:** ideas aren't worked out only in individual minds or in arguments. They are worked out in **social reality and history**, as societies face contradictions between social actors and find new ways to order their life together.

### Key vocabulary (see the reading guide)

- **Spirit / _Geist_** (sometimes "mind"; cf. _Zeitgeist_): a community's shared, living way of knowing and valuing. It includes both what it does without knowing it and what it is self-conscious about.
- **Ethical substance vs. law:** inherited custom (what people simply do, e.g., honoring the dead) vs. the community's explicit, self-conscious ordering of itself.
- **Immediate / reflective** (or **in-itself / for-itself**): the implicit and unexamined vs. the explicit and self-aware.
- **Moment:** not a point in time but one aspect of the process by which implicit things become explicit.
- **Negation / the negative:** not "bad." It's what limits and distinguishes one thing from another, a necessary part of anything.
- **Universal / particular / individual:** the law of the whole community / the role a person or group plays / the single person.
- **Human law vs. divine law:** the explicit, known law of the city (government) vs. unwritten laws that compel action just as strongly (the family, the dead, the underworld, what we do without knowing why).
    - Antigone acts from love and duty she doesn't need to explain. Creon has to state, explain, and justify his law out loud.

### Three guiding ideas for reading Hegel

1. Reason lives in a community and has a history. It isn't abstract.
2. Anything defined against its opposite turns out to contain, and often become, that opposite.
3. So a single community can split in two and destroy itself, which is what Hegel sees in _Antigone_.

### Was Hegel still an idealist?

- **One reading (dominant in the 19th c.):** history flows _necessarily_ toward the full realization of reason in a modern political order. This fits the century's other teleologies, e.g., Manifest Destiny and the _Communist Manifesto_'s inevitable proletarian revolution.
- **Another reading:** Hegel offers a critical, philosophical reading of how Western history produced _his_ present. That is a much more modest claim.
- Hegel was a Western chauvinist who saw Europe as the center of history. Still, there's a long tradition of extracting his **method** from any grand claim about history.

## The "Dialectic"

- "Dialectic" is an old term for argument that works through contradictions. It's often simplified as **thesis → antithesis → synthesis** (cf. Socrates in Plato's dialogues).
- That isn't really what Hegel does, and he didn't call his own method "dialectic." It's better understood through two philosophical problems:

**1. Identity and difference: what makes A = A?**

- We assume a thing's identity is self-contained. But try defining a tree without mentioning anything else: it's a tree partly because it _isn't_ a bush, a rock, or a lamppost.
- You are who you are through your differences from and relationships to others (child, friend, rival, student…).
- Hegel: identity and difference aren't opposites. Things become what they are _through_ their differences.

**2. The general and the specific (universal and particular)**

- Which is more real, "dog" in general or _this_ dog?
- To name a specific thing, you already need a general concept. A general concept with no instances is empty.
- Each needs the other. This is the same structure as identity and difference.

**In both cases:** two things we treat as separate and opposed turn out to belong together as parts of a larger whole.

## Hegel on _Antigone_

- **Not good vs. evil.** Antigone and Creon _each_ stand for something genuinely right:
    - **Antigone:** the family, the ethical substance of the community, and this particular brother's claim to burial no matter what he did.
    - **Creon:** the city, the law, the universal, rules that apply equally to everyone, even a king's relatives.
- **The tragedy is one-sidedness.** Each treats their side as the whole of what is right and refuses to recognize the other, even though each depends on the other.
- **A way of reading:** read both characters **generously** (they believe what they say) _and_ **suspiciously** (they are also asserting power). Each view is legitimate but partial.

### Each claim gets its force by excluding its opposite

- **Creon:** public office requires setting personal ties aside. (We still hold this today: we object when officials pardon or reward family members.)
- **Antigone:** mourning cares about the irreplaceable person, not their standing with the state. That Polynices attacked Thebes has to be excluded for her claim to work.

### The inversion

- _By needing to exclude what is different, each side depends on it._
- **Antigone**, defending private family devotion, becomes a **public actor**: a rebel against the king on behalf of an ideal she affirms for the whole city, and ultimately a martyr.
- **Creon**, defending impartial universal law, becomes a **particular, flawed man** who destroys his own family. He becomes a tyrant who represents no one but himself.
- "We know not what we do": social action makes people into the opposite of what they think they are. Tracing these inversions is the core of Hegel's method and a powerful tool for critiquing the status quo.

### A contemporary application: deporting parents of U.S.-born children

- **A Creon-like argument:** the law must apply equally. Exceptions for sympathetic stories turn law into favors.
- **An Antigone-like argument:** these are _specific_ parents and children. No abstract rule outweighs that bond, and the question of breaking immigration law has to be excluded for the claim to keep its force.
- **The inversions:** the "impartial" state comes to be seen through particular images (a crying child, a father detained at school drop-off), so it looks like particular power acting against particular people. Families defending private love become public actors (marches, lawsuits, rallies) making claims about justice for everyone.
- This **does not mean both sides are equally right.** The point is how positions transform through the very actions that make them real.

### Is there a resolution?

- Not one side winning, and not a halfway compromise. A new order in which **each claim is recognized inside the other**.
    - E.g., a city whose law guarantees burial to everyone, even traitors. Modern societies have largely resolved _this_ version of the conflict.
    - Modern democracy also separates family from the transfer of power, unlike the royal family in _Antigone_.
    - For immigration: family ties could be written into the law as something it weighs for everyone, rather than treated as exceptions for the most moving stories.
- **Not settled in thought alone.** Contradictions resolve only through real activity: acting, protesting, voting, building institutions.
- **Hegel's sobering addition:** every new order has new tensions, so the work of seeing what we exclude never ends.
