## Quick recap

This class discussion focused on Aeschylus’s Oresteia and its unresolved tension between personal vengeance and civic justice. The central debate concerned whether Orestes can be considered just or innocent after killing Clytemnestra: divine commands, family obligations, remorse, and an “eye-for-an-eye” tradition complicate the question, while the trial itself fails to establish an uncontested moral answer. The discussion ultimately treated the play’s movement from blood vengeance to courts not as a simple triumph of justice, but as an aporia: vengeance perpetuates destructive cycles, while state-administered justice can preserve existing structures of power rather than provide universal justice. The classes connected these problems to contemporary legal systems, systemic inequality, political power, wealth, and difficulties in achieving lasting reform. 12

## Summary

### Justice, Guilt, and the Cycle of Vengeance

The discussion examined whether Orestes’ killing of Clytemnestra could be justified even though the killing itself is acknowledged. Arguments considered divine instruction, obligations to avenge Agamemnon, the significance of matricide, and Orestes’ apparent psychological conflict. More broadly, the Oresteia portrays revenge as a debt that generates another debt: one killing may answer an earlier wrong while simultaneously creating grounds for further retaliation. This makes interpersonal vengeance emotionally compelling but incapable of creating lasting social stability. 32

### Gender, Family, and Power

Gender emerged as central to the competing definitions of justice. The Furies privilege the blood relationship between mother and child, while Apollo’s arguments diminish the maternal relationship and elevate paternal authority. Athena complicates this division: the resolution ends the immediate cycle of vengeance, but the court’s decision also reinforces the patriarchal organization of the city and household. The discussion therefore questioned whether replacing vengeance with law actually produces equality or instead converts existing social power into legal authority. 2

### Civic Justice and the Aporia

The transition to a court system transfers the authority to punish from injured individuals and families to the city. This can restrain endless retaliation, but it creates another problem: if the state and its laws reflect the interests of those already empowered, excluded groups may have no adequate means to pursue their own claims to justice. The play therefore leaves an unresolved tension between two inadequate alternatives—unstable person-to-person vengeance and an orderly civic system capable of institutionalizing exclusion. 132

### Justice, Wealth, and War

The classes also connected justice to debt, wealth, honor, and warfare. Ancient warfare was discussed as a system in which honor and material spoils were intertwined, while modern institutions were considered in relation to the continued influence of wealth and power. Similar concerns appeared in the discussion of contemporary criminal justice, including the capitalization of incarceration and differences in how petty offenses, wage theft, and white-collar wrongdoing are prosecuted. These examples reinforced the broader question of whether institutions identified with justice actually protect universal goods or reproduce established distributions of power. 132

### Modern Justice and Social Change

Contemporary parallels centered on systemic inequality and the difficulty of turning public demands for justice into structural reform. The discussion considered the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests, performative political responses, and the shrinking of physical “third spaces” that historically supported sustained organizing. Social media can mobilize people quickly but may struggle to maintain long-term movements. At the same time, the conversation resisted treating present arrangements as inevitable, emphasizing community organization and institutional change as interconnected avenues for transforming systems of power. 13

### Open Questions

- Can a justice system be genuinely fair when political, social, or economic power remains concentrated?
    
- Does civic law overcome vengeance, or merely suppress unresolved claims beneath a more stable political order?
    
- Should justice primarily punish past wrongdoing, prevent future harm, or accomplish both?
    
- When institutions reproduce the inequalities they were built around, is meaningful reform possible, or is more fundamental reconstruction necessary? 132
