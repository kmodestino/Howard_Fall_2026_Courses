## Art, justice, and the loss of a shared purpose

- For the Athenians, the purpose of tragedy was clear. Plays were performed at a religious festival before thousands of citizens and paid for as a public duty. The _Oresteia_ ends with Athena founding a real Athenian court, so the audience of the _Eumenides_ was watching the origin of the institution it lived under. **Theater, religion, and justice were bound together in one public occasion.**
- Theater emerged from religious ritual in many places, among them African mask drama, Noh, and Kabuki, and in those settings the gods or ancestors were understood to speak through the performance.
- By Shakespeare's time that bond was coming apart. England's religious drama, the **mystery plays**, which staged sacred history up to the Last Judgment, had been shut down as Catholic. In their place came **commercial theater**: paid admission, licensed by the crown, and attacked by preachers and Puritans as idle and corrupting.
- What a play is _for_ became an open question, and it still is. We go to plays and films by choice, for entertainment or personal aesthetic experience, not out of civic or religious duty.
- **Justice was changing too.** Older ways of settling guilt were ritual and sacred: oaths, confessions before God, the belief that heaven would reveal murderers. Over the early modern period, legal truth came to rest on **human evidence**: witnesses, observed behavior, probable inference, judges and juries weighing what they had seen.
- So art and justice both lose their ritual guarantees and run into the **same problem: how do you read truth from appearances when appearances can deceive?**

## The Mousetrap as experiment

- Hamlet won't act on the Ghost's word alone, which belongs to the old supernatural order of revelation. He wants "grounds more relative than this." So he designs _The Mousetrap_: a set test, two observers (Hamlet and Horatio), and a shared public conclusion.
- In discussion: Hamlet can't simply accuse a king without proof, so he makes the play into a kind of trial and casts himself as prosecutor, judge, and jury. Bringing Horatio in as a second witness suggests he doesn't fully trust his own judgment, knowing that grief and anger could distort it.
- Modern standards of evidence need more than one person's word, or a king's say-so. They need something repeatable, something others can attest to, and something displayed before a public.
- Theater is good at exactly this new kind of question. It shows conduct, faces, reactions, and **the gap between what people say and what they mean.** It can also show what evidence can't reach: in Act 3 the audience hears Claudius confess twice, and Hamlet, the would-be investigator, hears neither. His reading of Claudius at prayer is wrong. **The theater ends up knowing more than any procedure within the play, yet it has no power to act on what it knows.**

## "O, what a rogue and peasant slave am I" (2.2)

- The Player weeps "in a fiction, in a dream of passion... and all for nothing. For Hecuba! What's Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba?"
- This marks something new. In Greek drama, the figures represented were heroes and gods, objects of worship. A Christian weeping at a passion play would never say the tears were "for nothing." Here, acted emotion has full force and **no grounding in anything real.**
- Hamlet imagines the Player with _his_ motive would "drown the stage with tears." But Hamlet, who has the motive, displays nothing of the kind. He is his own evidence that real feeling and acted feeling come apart, and yet he wants to connect them.
- He then states two theories of how the play will work: that guilty creatures watching a play are "struck so to the soul" that they **proclaim** their crimes, or, more modestly, that he'll watch Claudius's face: "If he do blench, I know my course."
- **Question posed:** Are these good theories for finding stronger evidence? If you put someone's crimes before them in a play or a film, will that expose them?

## Can art serve as evidence?

- **Points raised in discussion:**
    - The method is unreliable. A murder on stage is disturbing to anyone, so an audience member's reaction may reflect the scene and not personal guilt.
    - Art reaches people that an argument or essay wouldn't. People choose to go and want to engage, they can picture it and feel it, and it makes something complicated easy to grasp.
    - Art is subjective: it may be true for one viewer and seem exaggerated to another. Art always tells the truth _of the artist_, but everyone interprets it differently.
    - Hamlet interrupts and narrates the play as it runs, forcing his own interpretation on the audience so they reach his conclusion.
    - The play may have damaged Hamlet more than Claudius, feeding his anger. Afterward he kills Polonius, treats Ophelia horribly, and swings wildly in his emotions.
    - Watching Claudius's reaction gives you something to work with, but not the full truth. And whoever produces the media controls how a situation gets interpreted.
- If art is subjective, **how can it function as proof?** A scientific experiment must be repeatable by anyone. A jury reaches a reasonable conclusion from public, shared evidence, not from a private response.
- That's why Hamlet acts like a **director**: "Speak the speech, I pray you... trippingly on the tongue"; don't "saw the air too much with your hand"; don't "tear a passion to tatters"; it "out-Herods Herod"; "Suit the action to the word, the word to the action." He instructs the players and Horatio, then keeps talking over the performance ("The lady doth protest too much, methinks" comes in the middle of it). Getting art to produce one precise effect is very hard.
- **Compare Orestes:** does he need this kind of proof before killing his mother? Hamlet wants proof that is absolute and incontrovertible. He chases certainty and tries to build it on one of the most slippery things there is: art.
- And yet we regularly place faith in art to bring hidden things to light. A loosely autobiographical story of someone facing racism, sexism, or other oppression matters as their experience, but we also treat it as testimony to something bigger than one person. A film about wrongdoing by the powerful would never stand up in a courtroom, but it still counts as evidence in some special way.
- Art also works as **historical evidence**, for example political cartoons showing how the public saw people in power.
- **Revenge vs. justice:** justice would mean Claudius held accountable, and consequences don't have to mean death. Hamlet sparing Claudius at prayer so he won't go to heaven suggests something other than worldly justice is driving him. His focus on avenging his father blinds him to the damage he does to everyone around him.

## Censorship, self-censorship, and propaganda

- **Points raised in discussion:** How can art fulfill its purpose if it's censored, for instance when social media posts are taken down? Growing awareness of censorship may push people to build new ways of sharing work.
- Who controls the medium through which art reaches us is a huge question. **Every one of Shakespeare's plays had to pass a royal censor** before it could be performed, and he still built a reputation with plays that were sometimes sharply critical of power. The powerful try to control art because they believe it can do something.
- Shakespeare's history plays raise the question of art as **pro-royal propaganda**, and of where he may have slipped more complicated ideas about royalty and power into them.
- **Self-censorship:** in discussion, the "To be, or not to be" scene came up. Hamlet speaks freely because he doesn't know Polonius and Claudius are watching, while we censor ourselves because we know we're watched. Words like "unalive" have become normal speech because people anticipate algorithmic suppression. **Much censorship in modern society is self-censorship.** High-surveillance and authoritarian societies don't always need to censor actively, because a maze of self-censorship builds up on its own.
- Hamlet lives in that maze. He is trying to say "you killed my father" without saying it, unsure who is watching or whom he can trust, and he puts on an "antic disposition" so no one will suspect that he suspects.
- **Propaganda:** a point raised in discussion was that propaganda gives you one story to follow. Even the Ghost gives Hamlet a single story with no other perspective. This anticipates **Du Bois**, who argues that all art is propaganda and that there needs to be a positive propaganda as part of the struggle for Black rights.
- **Walter Benjamin**, a German Jewish left-wing philosopher who fled the Nazis: "Only that historian will have the gift of fanning the spark of hope in the past who is firmly convinced that even the dead will not be safe from the enemy if he wins. And this enemy has not ceased to be victorious."
    - Power lies about the past as well as the present, and it takes figures associated with movements and makes them mean something else. **Martin Luther King Jr.** is often domesticated into a safer consensus figure, but he was radically anti-war, pro–working people, and organizing the Poor People's Campaign.
    - Claudius does the same thing. Power establishes itself in _Hamlet_ by telling you what the past meant, including the memory of Hamlet's father. Resisting means wresting the past free from those who would rewrite it.

## Revealing injustice vs. producing justice

- **Points raised in discussion:** Exposing the truth doesn't by itself achieve justice. Hamlet gets his confirmation, and destruction follows anyway. Art may be a gateway to justice, not justice itself: it creates knowledge, recognition, and pressure that make justice possible. The play backfires on Hamlet. Media and celebrity can also serve as a distraction, or as a test of what the public will tolerate.
- Once art reveals a crime, then **what?** The line from revelation to justice isn't clear, and for Hamlet it's hopelessly confused: he ends up hurting many people who didn't commit the crime.
- _Do the Right Thing_ protests police violence and brings a community's experience to light. It's a vital film, and it has not stopped police violence. Nobody asks a film to do that alone, but we still have to ask where we are once the truth has been spoken.
- Art also **creates a community of people who recognize it.** Maybe that's part of what Hamlet wants: a community of witnesses. He doesn't get there, but that doesn't make the goal unreal.
- What if the audience saw the play and shrugged? Instead of bringing about justice, art can be met with indifference.

## "To be, or not to be" (3.1)

- In discussion: Hamlet weighs whether it's easier to live or to die, choosing between a suffering life and the uncertainty of death. He compares death to sleep.
- In Christian theology, life is suffering in the interim before judgment and redemption, and death redeems "the heartache and the thousand natural shocks / That flesh is heir to." The speech is a famous expression of **modern doubt** because Hamlet lacks that certainty about what death means: "the undiscovered country from whose bourn / No traveller returns."
- This is also a question of **proof**. If everything you believe has to rest on the evidence of your senses, where does that leave death and the afterlife?
- The speech is also an extraordinary act of **linguistic invention**: "slings and arrows of outrageous fortune," "sea of troubles," "perchance to dream," "what dreams may come," "this mortal coil," "the whips and scorns of time." Isn't that verve itself an affirmation of life? The play leaves the contradiction between profound despair and overwhelming creativity unresolved.
- **Question posed:** If Hamlet is cursed to live in a world where the justice he seeks will never come, what is his consolation? Is that the gap art enters? _Do the Right Thing_ didn't end the injustices it depicts, but it exists. It spoke those things and gave a vision of a Brooklyn community, with its joy, culture, wit, and music.

## The theater and the courtroom

- Courtroom dramas are everywhere: the _Eumenides_ (the first courtroom drama), _To Kill a Mockingbird_, _Twelve Angry Men_. A real courtroom is mostly boring procedure, but it has a **dramaturgy**: prosecutors, defendants, and judges perform their roles, and hidden things come to light.
- _Hamlet_ is the inverse: the stage made into a kind of court. Think about how these two spaces relate as modes of evidence.
- That thread continues into **Baldwin's _Blues for Mister Charlie_**, much of which takes place in a courtroom, and into Du Bois on art and propaganda.
