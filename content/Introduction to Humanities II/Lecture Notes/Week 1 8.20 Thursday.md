## Session Overview

This session combined student introductions with a substantive lecture on Aristotle's Poetics, focusing on mimesis, plot unity, and tragic structure. Professor Modestino covered Chapters 8–9, walked through Sophocles' Oedipus Rex as Aristotle's model of perfect tragic unity, and previewed Spike Lee's Do the Right Thing for next week. Students applied Aristotelian concepts — particularly the unity of plot — to contemporary works during an open discussion period.

## Core Lecture Topics

- Mimesis as the active imitation of a single unified action, not a passive copy of reality.
- Plot unity (Chapter 8): A plot must center on one action to which all elements are necessary. Homer's Odyssey omits Odysseus's wound and feigned madness to focus on a single action: the restoration of his household. Unity is not about following one character's whole life.
- Poesis ("making"): The poet is a maker of plots — imposing unity and cause-and-effect clarity on disordered experience.
- Chekhov's Gun cited as a descendant of Aristotelian necessity: if a gun appears in Act 1, it must fire in Act 3.
- Tragedy (Chapter 9): Aims to produce pity and fear through events that are simultaneously surprising and inevitable. The two mechanisms: reversal of fortune and recognition.
- Oedipus Rex as Aristotle's model of perfect tragic unity — reversal and recognition arrive simultaneously on a single day. Also serves as backstory for Antigone (assigned later in the semester).
- Biopic example: A Complete Unknown (Bob Dylan) praised as a focused biopic anchored to a single action — Dylan's electric pivot at Newport Folk Festival.
- Preview: Spike Lee's Do the Right Thing (1989) next week — Greek dramatic unity of time (one day), unity of place (one Brooklyn block), and a DJ chorus figure (Samuel L. Jackson). Lee's Chi-Raq (2015) adapts Aristophanes' Lysistrata to Chicago gun violence.
