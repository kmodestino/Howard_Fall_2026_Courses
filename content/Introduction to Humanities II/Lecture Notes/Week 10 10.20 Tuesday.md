# Humanities II — Class Notes, October 20, 2026

## _Hamlet_, Acts 1–2

---

## Recap: Aristotle and Locke

- For **Aristotle**, just action lives in community: reciprocity, character formed by habit, practical wisdom, and **equity** (correcting the law where its generality fails a particular case).
- **Locke** compresses justice into one faculty: individual judgment by "calm reason and conscience." But he admits we can't be trusted with it, since self-love, passion, and revenge carry us too far.
- **The bind:** the modern self can judge everything but has almost no warrant to act. Family, tradition, and community, which gave action meaning for Aristotle, are now suspect as partiality. **This is Hamlet's situation.**

---

## Acts 1–2

- **Fortinbras and Laertes** are sons who act on claims of justice; Hamlet is the son who can't.
- **Claudius** runs a model legal order: counsel, consent, diplomacy instead of war. He lets Laertes go to France but **refuses to let Hamlet return to Wittenberg**.
- Before he knows of any crime, Hamlet already judges everything around him and has no way to act: "break, my heart, for I must hold my tongue."
- **"To thine own self be true"** is ironic: Polonius is a blowhard who then spies on his own son, in a play where the self is hidden and hard to know.
- **The Ghost** gives Hamlet Orestes' command, but without the gods, chorus, or inherited code that made Orestes' act intelligible. With the murderer on the throne, there's no judge to appeal to.
- **Act 2 is a court of surveillance**, and **"the play's the thing / Wherein I'll catch the conscience of the king"** is an attempt to build an evidentiary procedure where no court exists.

---

## Discussion

- **What does Hamlet lack that would let him act? Is the problem his psychology or the world he's in?** Discussion pointed to:
    - lingering hope that his uncle is innocent
    - not wanting to become a murderer
    - grief and instability
    - the lack of any law that could act against a king
    - an attachment to his own performance (one Lacanian reading: we can enjoy our symptoms too much to give them up)
- **Is he refusing out of moral horror, or to escape responsibility?**
- **Evidence and the hidden self.** Greek revenge happens in public. Shakespeare asks whether we are the roles we play, and how we could know anyone's real self. The play withholds answers about the Ghost and about Gertrude.
- **Manipulation:** Hamlet is always the smartest person on stage, which doesn't make him the just one. Ophelia, Gertrude, and Rosencrantz and Guildenstern bear the collateral damage.
- **Grief** is the Lockean impossibility. It may be how Hamlet knows a wrong has been done, yet it disqualifies him from acting justly. In the Greek world, grief was the claim of the dead calling you to act; for Hamlet it's something to distrust.

---

## Close Reading: 1.2

- **Claudius's speech** ("mirth in funeral… dirge in marriage, / In equal scale weighing delight and dole") is a good public performance of a king stabilizing a state at war. **As a brother**, though, it's suspiciously measured. Then again, how could anyone in public life prove sufficient mourning?
- **"A little more than kin, and less than kind"; "I am too much in the sun"; "Ay, madam, it is common."** Hamlet answers with bitter puns, and his mother misses the second meaning.
- **"I have that within which passes show."** Everyone else performs grief; Hamlet claims to feel it.
- Claudius then publicly calls his grief "unmanly" and "a fault to heaven," tells him to think of Claudius as his father, and still won't let him leave. It's a **ritual public shaming**, and it sets up the first soliloquy.
