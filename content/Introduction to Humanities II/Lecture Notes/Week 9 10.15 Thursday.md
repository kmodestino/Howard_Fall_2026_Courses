## Context

- **Hamlet the character is what makes the play unique.** The revenge plot itself is common: a throne usurped by an uncle, a son who returns for justice. We saw a version of it in the _**Oresteia**_, and the Mande epic _**Sundiata**_ has a similar shape. But Hamlet is charismatic, funny, flawed, and horrible all at once, and he's one of Shakespeare's richest characters.
- **Shakespeare wrote popular entertainment.** The Globe was an open-air theater in London's red-light district. Its audiences were rowdy and mostly stood (the **groundlings**), and they often came for the clown. Shakespeare was also a businessman and a part-owner of the Globe.
- **Politics and religion.** Plays had to pass a royal censor, and an aging Elizabeth with no heir made a play about a murdered king touchy. The Ghost is a spirit of **purgatory**, a Catholic doctrine the Church of England had rejected, and he demands vengeance from his son. That made the play theologically and politically risky. Hamlet studies at **Wittenberg**, where Luther posted his 95 Theses, which puts a Protestant student face to face with a Catholic ghost.

## How to Read Shakespeare

- **Read it aloud.** It's a script. A performance can clear up a confusing passage.
- **Iambic pentameter** has five beats per line of unstressed-stressed ("un-FOLD your-SELF"), a natural rhythm for English. Irregular beats often mark important moments.
- **Follow the punctuation, not the line breaks**, and **find the subject and verb** when word order is inverted.
- **"You" vs. "thou":** "you" is formal and respectful. "Thou" can signal intimacy, speaking down, or contempt.
- Watch for familiar words used in unfamiliar ways, and pay attention to **puns**.
- **Soliloquies** give us a character's **interiority**, which was new in theater. Hamlet hides himself from everyone on stage but lets the audience in, almost seducing us onto his side. That doesn't mean Shakespeare wants us won over.

## Act 1, Scene 1

- **"Who's there?"** On a daylight stage with minimal sets, the language has to create darkness and suspense. It may also be the play's central question: who are you, and how do I know?
- **"Stand and unfold yourself."** In discussion, we read "unfold" as revealing your whole identity, proving your legitimacy, and showing more depth than "reveal" would. It also hints at deception. You also unfold clothes, which raises the play's question of whether people are who they appear to be.
- **"Long live the king!"** is the watchword. But who is the king?
- **"I am sick at heart."** Francisco is on edge, and we don't know why.
- **"The rivals of my watch"**: "rivals" means partners. The blurring of the two words hints that the danger lies inside the kingdom, not outside it.

## Hamlet's First Soliloquy (1.2)

- Hamlet wishes suicide weren't a sin and finds the world an "unweeded garden." He's angry that his mother remarried within a month.
- **Take the grief seriously.** His father is dead.
- **The soliloquy is dramatic.** Hamlet hears himself think and reacts, and the speech dramatizes ideas developing.
- **Watch where his thinking goes:** from existential despair to a fixation on his mother's sexuality ("incestuous sheets"). Hamlet is **misogynistic**. He turns his pain into cruelty toward Gertrude, whose guilt is an open question, and toward Ophelia, who is guilty of nothing.
- **"No more like my father / Than I to Hercules."** Hamlet isn't Hercules either. Like the modern self we discussed with **Locke**, he can see, judge, and articulate everything but sees himself as **unable to act**.
