## What the Oral Defense Asks of You

- In class comments and one-on-one meetings, I give participation points for insightful commentary that shows you understand the text, even if it isn't a formal argument. **The oral defense is different:** you need an **interpretive argument** that is arguable, that you have prepared, that you can support with evidence, and that I can ask you questions about. You won't get all three points without that.
- The oral defense isn't reading the play, putting together a summary, and walking me through it. It's spending the time to develop a thesis and a couple of pieces of evidence, and being ready to defend them when I ask questions.

---

## Summary vs. Interpretation

- **A summary answers "What does the text say?"** An **interpretation answers "Why this, here, in this way?"** Why is the text saying this in this moment? Does it complicate our understanding? Is it something people might miss, and if so, what does noticing it add?
- **Don't try to account for the whole text.** That's usually a sign you have a summary, not an interpretive argument. The only claim that covers everything in a text is a restatement of it in your own words. Summary has its place (in an essay, for example), but it's not what's being asked here.
- **Interpretations usually start from something small:** one line, one word, one piece of characterization, or one speech you think is significant and needs attention.

### The test: is it contestable?

- **1. Someone could reasonably disagree.** An intelligent reader could come to a different conclusion from the same evidence. You don't have to agree with that counterargument or even bring it up; it just has to be conceivable. A good summary is one every careful reader would accept. Even a detailed, nuanced summary is something everyone would agree with once you pointed out the detail.
    - Example: many characters in Greek plays tell you their motivation directly. Repeating that motivation isn't an argument, because no one would contest it.
- **2. It can be supported.** It isn't a feeling, a vibe, or an impression. It rests on evidence other people can look at, and they could potentially come to agree with how you read it. In other words, it's **adjudicable based on evidence**.
- Put together: a reasonable reader could resist your claim, but you have a good argument to support it.

## Two Examples

These examples show that the difference isn't about phrasing. Each "summary" is phrased as an argument and still isn't one.

### Antigone

- **Summary phrased as an argument:** "In this essay, I want to argue that Antigone's defiance of Creon is ultimately a defense of divine law against human authority. Although Creon claims the power of the state, Antigone demonstrates that the unwritten laws of the gods, which demand burial for the dead, must take precedence over any mortal decree."
    - This just restates what Antigone herself says, more than once, in the play. You can support it with the text, but no one could contest it.
- **Interpretation:** Near her death, Antigone says she would not have defied the city for a husband or a child, since those could be replaced, but her brother cannot be. In this moment her justification shrinks from universal divine law to one irreplaceable family bond, **which suggests that the earlier appeal to the gods may not have been her real ground.**
    - This starts from a strange, significant detail and makes a claim about it: that this statement, made in crisis, is her real belief, rather than what she said earlier.
    - Someone could disagree: her real beliefs might lie elsewhere, or the two justifications might not be in opposition at all. The meaning of the detail isn't settled. It has to be argued, and this is one option.

### Hamlet

- **Summary phrased as an argument:** "I will argue that the prayer scene is a crucial example of Hamlet's tragic indecision. By refusing to kill Claudius at prayer for fear of sending him to heaven, Hamlet reveals that his deepest flaw is his inability to act, which ultimately leads to his downfall."
    - This focuses on a scene, but no one would argue that Hamlet isn't indecisive. He is, very evidently, throughout the play, and we've discussed it at length in class.
- **Interpretation:** Hamlet calls killing a praying man "hire and salary, not revenge," as if revenge had to be an exact repayment. **That demand for perfect reciprocity is what stops him.** Right after he leaves, Claudius admits his prayer failed. So the delay rests on a misreading by the same man who just used the play to read Claudius's guilt.
    - This gets at something much finer than the general theme of indecision: that interpretation and misinterpretation are vital to the play and its vision of justice, a big but not always acknowledged theme.
    - Someone could disagree: the play-within-the-play might itself be a misreading of Claudius's guilt; Claudius's sense that the prayer failed might not matter because it's still a prayer; or the scene might be about religion and belief rather than interpretation.

### What separates them

- The first statement in each pair **sounds like a thesis**. It says "I argue," uses words like "demonstrates" and "reveals," and makes an evaluative claim. But it only restates what the play openly says (Antigone's own stated reasons) or what everyone already believes about it (Hamlet is indecisive). Nothing in it depends on a detail the student actually noticed.
- The interpretation is **different in kind, not just in wording.** It starts from something you can point to, reaches a claim about the whole work through that detail, and creates tension with obvious or more superficial readings.
- **If the author would gladly accept your interpretation as a description of their own work, it may still be a summary**, however argumentative it sounds.

## Format and Grading

- **Length:** your presentation should take **3 to 5 minutes**, not 30. It isn't exhaustive. It's: here is my interpretive claim, here are a couple of pieces of evidence, here is how that evidence supports it.
- The meetings so far have run **about 10 to 15 minutes** total, including questions.
- **Rubric (3 points):**
    - **1 point:** your claim is actually a claim (it's arguable).
    - **1 point:** you have evidence to support it.
    - **1 point:** you can respond to a couple of questions.
- **Questions:** yes, I will ask you questions about your claim. This isn't rigorous questioning, and I'm not trying to disprove or undermine your claim. My questions usually push your argument further in one direction or another. If you show facility with the text and can respond to what I'm asking, that's the point.
- **Know it beyond your script.** Be ready to talk about it in a way that goes beyond the sentences of argument and evidence you've prepared: have some ideas about other parts of the play that might come up and possible counterarguments to your point.
- **Arguable matters more than "right."** Worry more that your claim isn't arguable than that it's wrong. There are unsupported claims, but not wrong ones. A very counterintuitive interpretation is fine if you have evidence that does a good job supporting it.
- You're in an intro humanities class, so work with the text in front of you. You don't need big contextual claims.
- **Open notes.** You can write it out and have it ready.
- I'll **tell you how you did at the end of the meeting.**
- There are no take-home writing assignments and no final exam in this class. This is it, so treat it with that level of seriousness in your preparation.
- I can't do a pre-meeting to test whether your thesis is good enough before the real thing, but I'm happy to talk through any of this material in detail in one-on-ones.

## On Using AI

- I can't stop you from using AI to assist, and the oral defense isn't meant to police that. The point of the exercise isn't to test whether you used AI. It's to test whether you have spent the time developing a claim and can support it under pressure.
- If AI writes the argument for you and you don't understand what you're saying, it will be obvious in the oral defense. You'll be asked to think on the fly a little, and if you haven't thought it through yourself, you'll struggle.
- If you're unsure whether you've developed an interpretive claim, you could pose it to an AI and see what it says, but don't take its feedback as gospel. Talking to classmates during your prep is another option.
- For transparency: I used AI to help come up with the Antigone and Hamlet examples above, because I wanted clear examples of the distinction.

## Why This Matters

- The whole idea of criteria-based grading is that there's a set of criteria and I want everyone in the class to meet them. I want you all to get all three points.
- Learning what makes a good argument and good evidence for it is an important skill in life: not just restating what everyone says and thinks, but doing the work to develop your own perspective and interpretation. That doesn't come just from reading something; you have to study it.
- I've seen this skill develop in a lot of you this semester, in class discussion and in one-on-one meetings. The oral defense is the final test of it.
