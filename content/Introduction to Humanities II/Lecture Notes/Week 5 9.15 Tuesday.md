## Key Outcomes

The session established the Humanities as a rigorous academic discipline focused on understanding particular, unique human events rather than discovering universal predictive laws. The class then shifted from philosophical analysis to literary analysis, introducing tropes—especially metaphor—as tools for interpreting Aeschylus’ Agamemnon. 12

## The Nature of the Humanities

- Distinction from STEM/Social Sciences: Sciences generally seek explanatory or predictive laws, while the Humanities emphasize understanding the particular, idiosyncratic, and historically or culturally specific.
    
- Truth Claims: Humanistic scholarship makes evidence-based truth claims but does not require those claims to take the form of universal laws.
    
- Pluralism: The Humanities begin from a baseline pluralism, attending to differences among human experiences rather than reducing them to a single theory of behavior.
    
- Methodology: There is no single humanist method. Methods respond to the objects being studied; this can be understood as a practice of redescription—attending closely to particulars in order to articulate their meanings. 12
    

## Literary Analysis and Tropes

- Philosophy vs. Literature: Philosophy can proceed through definitions, propositions, logical analysis, and syllogisms, while literary works use devices such as plot, drama, imagery, and figurative language to explore meaning.
    
- Definition of Trope: From Greek tropos, meaning “turn,” a trope occurs when a word or expression is “turned” away from its conventional literal meaning.
    
- Master Tropes:
    
    - Metonymy: Substituting something through a real association—for example, “Benjamins” for $100 bills.
    - Synecdoche: Using a part to represent a whole—for example, $100 bills standing for money more generally.
    - Irony/Sarcasm: Saying one thing while meaning its opposite.
    - Metaphor: Bringing together fundamentally unlike things in order to discover an unexpected similarity. 12

## Mechanics of Metaphor

- Conventional vs. Poetic Metaphor: Some metaphors become so conventional that they are barely noticed (“dead metaphors”). Poetic metaphors are more “impertinent”: they violate ordinary expectations about which things can meaningfully be compared.
    
- Producing New Thought: Because metaphor joins unlike things, it can create new ways of thinking rather than merely restating an existing idea.
    
- Contradiction: Metaphor can sustain relationships that would be contradictory in formal logic: in a metaphorical sense, A can both be B and not literally be B. This capacity makes metaphor particularly useful for poetic and other forms of thought that cannot be reduced to straightforward propositions. 12
    

## Application to Aeschylus’ Agamemnon

- The Net Metaphor: The recurring image of the net provides a way to think about justice in the play.
    
- Volition and Inevitability: The net captures a central tension: it must be cast by someone, making justice an intentional act, yet it can also represent an inescapable fate.
    
- Plot and Metaphor: Aeschylus reinforces the metaphor through Clytemnestra’s use of a net-like fabric to trap Agamemnon before killing him. The figurative image thus becomes part of the action of the play itself. 1
