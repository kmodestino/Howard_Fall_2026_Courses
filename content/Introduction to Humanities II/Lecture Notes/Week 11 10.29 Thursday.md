## Context: Du Bois, the NAACP, and the Harlem Renaissance

- "Criteria of Negro Art" (1926) was a speech given at an NAACP event honoring the historian **Carter G. Woodson**. It's often anthologized with the **Harlem Renaissance**, and often set against statements by **Zora Neale Hurston**.
- The Harlem Renaissance was a real flourishing of Black art in the 1920s: **Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, Alain Locke, Countee Cullen, Claude McKay**, Duke Ellington in the Harlem clubs, and later movements in Chicago's Bronzeville and elsewhere.
- But it didn't emerge fully formed. Du Bois and the **NAACP** had spent decades building magazines, awards, publication, and audiences for Black art. That gave the movement a framework not wholly controlled by white publishers and patrons, though some of the Renaissance did have white patrons.
- Du Bois was **ambivalent** about the Renaissance. He saw it as the fruition of his work, but he was also trying to steer it. He wanted aspirational art: domestic scenes, spirituals, beauty. He worried that too much of it was "sex, drugs, and jazz."
- Not everyone agreed with him. **Claude McKay** rejected Du Bois's view of what Black art should be. Hurston wrote about poor and rural people, folk tales, Haitian Vodou, and sexuality, using dialect, which is close to the opposite of what Du Bois wanted. There was a strain of **respectability politics** in his position at this point, though his ideas are more complex than that, and he later turned hard toward Marxism and the working class.
- Hurston's reputation was revived in the 1970s when **Alice Walker** helped bring her work back into print. _Their Eyes Were Watching God_ is now widely taught.
- In discussion we connected this to _The New Negro_, the landmark Harlem Renaissance anthology edited by **Alain Locke**, who was a longtime professor here at Howard.
- Du Bois wrote across many genres: essays, music criticism, speeches, sociology, history, and short fiction, including what you might call the first Afrofuturist short story.

## Context: _Birth of a Nation_ and Reconstruction

- _Rachel_ was written in response to an NAACP call for works of art to counter **D. W. Griffith's _The Birth of a Nation_** (1915), the most popular film in the U.S. at the time and an extremely racist one. It was adapted from Thomas Dixon's novel _**The Clansman**_.
- The film tells the story of the Civil War and Reconstruction from a white Southern perspective and celebrates the **Ku Klux Klan**. It portrays Black people as violent and the Klan as heroes.
- **Reconstruction** was a high-water mark of Black political power in the South: Black members of Congress, the Freedmen's Bureau, schools. That representation was flawed but real, and federal troops held racial terror at bay.
- Reconstruction "failed" when Northern capitalists made a deal with Southern planters to withdraw the troops. The first Klan then used **terrorist violence** to destroy Black political power, which produced the **Jim Crow** order of segregation and disenfranchisement. Jim Crow is not what the Civil War ended with. It was imposed through terror.
- Afterward, schools, universities, and popular culture taught a false story of Reconstruction as corrupt, doomed "carpetbagger" rule, with the Klan as restorer of order. _Birth of a Nation_ dramatizes that story.
- The film's release helped revive the Klan, especially in **Northern cities** where Black migrants had settled, so the terror followed people north. **Woodrow Wilson** screened it at the White House.
- Discussion points: the film was also a landmark of filmmaking technique, and many of its methods became norms of Hollywood. That makes it one of the hardest and most necessary truths about American film history. It is propaganda in the purest sense: a powerful group lying about history to justify its violence.
- Du Bois fought the false history of Reconstruction his whole career. In the 1930s he published _**Black Reconstruction in America**_, which debunked it and transformed how historians understand the period.

## Context: Angelina Weld Grimké

- _Rachel_ was the **first play by a Black woman produced in the U.S. with an all-Black cast**. The NAACP sponsored it, and it was first staged **here in Washington, DC**. Grimké was also a published poet and short-story writer.
- The play's city is deliberately unnamed. It could be any city the **Great Migration** reached: DC, Baltimore, Chicago, New York.
- Family history: her father was born into slavery in Charleston, South Carolina. His father was the white enslaver, and his mother was an enslaved woman. The enslaver's sisters were the **Grimké sisters**, white Southern abolitionists who left the South and were estranged from their slaveholding brother. They wrote about slavery's horrors, including its destruction of families and motherhood. After emancipation they helped his children move north and establish themselves. Angelina Weld Grimké is named after one of them.
- There's a long line here connecting **abolitionism** to the **anti-lynching** protest of the early 20th century.
- A line of influence can be drawn from _Rachel_ to **Lorraine Hansberry's _A Raisin in the Sun_**.

## Du Bois: "All Art Is Propaganda"

- **Discussion points:**
    - Du Bois argues that Black people are treated as lesser yet never given the chance to show otherwise. That ties back to our _Hamlet_ question of whether art matters, and to why the NAACP would care about art alongside voting rights. Du Bois gives a strong answer to the question _Hamlet_ raised about art's role in the pursuit of justice.
    - Art is a form of power: whoever controls how a group is represented shapes how society sees it. Du Bois notes that Black performers were accepted when they fit white stereotypes (comic roles, for instance) but had little room to represent themselves truthfully.
    - Du Bois warns that the success of a few Black artists doesn't mean racism is over, and that individual success is often used today to argue that opportunity is equal.
    - Both _Hamlet_ and Du Bois use art to reveal truth. In _Hamlet_ the play exposes guilt. Du Bois is more direct: art should expose injustice.
    - People often dismiss art as "just entertainment" to deny responsibility for it. By calling art propaganda, Du Bois names it as a political speech act with ethical consequences.
- **The key passage (paragraph 26):** "Thus all art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists." Du Bois says whatever art he has has always been used to gain "the right of black folk to love and enjoy," and that what he objects to is propaganda "confined to one side while the other is stripped and silent."
- **The question I posed:** What does "propaganda" mean when you first hear it, and what does Du Bois mean by it?
    - The word carries baggage. Especially from the 1950s on, Americans associated it with Soviet state art: lying, manipulative, simplistic, a fresh coat of paint over messy realities. Even at the time, some artists, like McKay, didn't want their work called propaganda.
    - Answers from discussion: art always influences how people see and act, for good or bad; and propaganda is the forwarding of a particular message or ideology.
    - Du Bois means something more complex. The point isn't only what a work says. Creating beauty is itself a testament to the humanity of the people who make it and appear in it. **Beauty itself militates on behalf of truth and justice.**
- **Beauty (paragraph 7):** Du Bois names four beautiful things: the cathedral at Cologne, a village in West Africa, the Venus de Milo, and a phrase of music from the South (a spiritual). He says the mass of people are "choked away" from beauty. _Why these four things?_
- **Paragraph 28:** Du Bois objects not to white propaganda as such but to denying Black people the same right of self-representation. He argues that white artists suffer from this narrowing too. **DuBose Heyward** can write _Porgy_ (later the basis of the opera _Porgy and Bess_) about the Black Charleston underworld because he couldn't write the same way about white Charleston without being "drummed out of town." Art that must present white people as always perfect is itself constrained and can't reach complexity.
- Du Bois isn't asking for only positive images. His examples include tragedy and hopes denied by racial prejudice. He wants art that is **beautiful by being true**, complex and truer than white America's projections. It's an almost Romantic idea of the power of beauty, but for him beauty is part of the world, not outside it.
- He's answering the late-19th-century **"art for art's sake"** position: you can have art for art's sake once the world is free and just. Until then, beauty serves a purpose.
- **Connections to the Oresteia and _Hamlet_ from discussion:**
    - The _Oresteia_ can be read as propaganda for Athens and its court system, even while it questions what justice means.
    - _Hamlet_ can't be reduced to a simple message, but England has still used it as propaganda for Englishness and the English language.
    - The _Oresteia_ has gods, a court, and a community to give Orestes' actions meaning. Hamlet is isolated and skeptical, and his play-within-the-play exposes truth rather than establishing a shared political order.
    - How a society moves from an unjust order to a just one is a major subject of art.
- **History as art:** Du Bois was also a historian. He asks why Napoleonic Europe and the French Revolution get romantic, heroic treatment while African history, with as much tragedy and heroism, goes unnamed. We get a fuller picture of history when the stories that have been overshadowed are told.

## Representation, Audience, and Fetishization

- **Discussion points:**
    - Today's media often presents Black life through violence and unstable families. A story about someone who went to Howard and became a doctor would also be the truth, and a positive one.
    - Du Bois's point is that white America can't admit to its own poor, so it projects those stories onto Black people. In the 1990s, gangster rap's biggest audience was white suburban kids. Hip-hop moved from the political edge of 1980s artists like Eric B. & Rakim and Public Enemy to crime stories that sold to a mainstream white audience. None of that sums up what those artists achieved, but it's part of the history Du Bois anticipates.
    - Rap is now arguably the largest global medium of pop culture and messaging. What responsibility comes with that? Du Bois couldn't have imagined hip-hop as a global, billion-dollar enterprise, but the question is embedded in his essay.
    - Black art is often accepted only when it fits a white lens. Beyoncé's _Lemonade_, which addressed heritage and police violence, unsettled audiences who had known her as an R&B and pop star. Authentic art may make society uncomfortable.
    - Radio Raheem in _Do the Right Thing_: Black pride that wasn't made "digestible."
    - Black artists face pressure to conform to what platforms and white audiences will reward in order to make a living.
- **Is it fetishization?** Fetishization is a decent concept for what's going on, and it didn't start with hip-hop:
    - In the 1920s, white audiences took the A train up to Harlem for the jazz clubs without fully seeing jazz as art. Jazz musicians, Duke Ellington among them, had to fight to be received as serious artists.
    - In the 1960s and '70s, white musicians described Jimi Hendrix as "raw expression" rather than as an artist mastering a craft.
    - Du Bois doesn't have the word, but he identifies the success of _Porgy_ as fetishistic.

## _Rachel_, Act I

- **Discussion points:**
    - Rachel seems whimsical, dreamy, and childlike, especially around children, but something deeper surfaces when she talks about children being harmed.
    - Her maternal love, her nurturing, and the clash between her dreams and the reality of racism.
- Act I is a warm portrait of family and community in an apartment building in a Northern city, among people who fled the South and found a place. The family is named the **Lovings**. It's not a subtle play, but it is a powerful one, and in many ways what Du Bois calls for.
- The day of the play is the anniversary of the deaths of Mrs. Loving's husband and her older son George (Rachel and Tom's half-brother). At the end of the act she finally tells her children what happened (p. 24):
    - Their father owned and edited a small Black newspaper and was "utterly fearless."
    - When a mob of "the respectable people of the town" lynched a Black man they knew to be innocent, he published a denunciation, refused an anonymous demand to retract it, and published an even stronger one.
    - Masked men came to the house. He was killed, and so was George when he tried to defend him. That's why the family fled north.
- The play exposes racial terror as a way of **suppressing truth**: covering for white men's crimes and silencing those who spoke out. The Jim Crow order was a reign of terror maintained by violence.
- **The mother's shame:** why would she feel shame over a death that was a noble act? I'm leaving that open for discussion. One reading from class: it isn't only that her husband died. Her child was killed, and she's a mother who couldn't protect him. Protecting children is a major theme of the play.
- **The home isn't a refuge.** As in Greek drama, the violence happens offstage, but racial oppression doesn't stop at the door. It shapes family life, desires, and relationships. _A Raisin in the Sun_ works the same way: no white characters on stage, and the whole play inside the home, yet it's about segregation and redlining.
- **A feminist dimension:** the domestic, "women's" sphere is part of politics, not outside it. Rachel lacks a clear space to act in, which implies an argument for extending political action to women.
- **Connections to watch for as you read:**
    - **Antigone:** grief that becomes political or needs a political outlet; justice denied that goes underground and haunts a society; a sphere coded as non-political that tyrannical power doesn't leave alone.
    - **Hamlet:** the desire for justice with no clear sphere or context for action. Some of Rachel's monologues echo and rework Hamlet's sentiments.
