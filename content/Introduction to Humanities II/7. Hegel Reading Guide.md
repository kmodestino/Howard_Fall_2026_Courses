# Reading Guide: Hegel on Antigone and the Ethical World

## What you are reading

G. W. F. Hegel, _The Phenomenology of Mind_ (1807), trans. J. B. Baillie (1910), Chapter VI, "Spirit," section A, "Objective Spirit: The Ethical Order":

- (a) ["The Ethical World: Law Human and Divine: Man and Woman," §§446–63](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hegel/works/ph/phc2aa.htm)
- (b) ["Ethical Action: Knowledge Human and Divine: Guilt and Destiny," §§464–76](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hegel/works/ph/phc2ab.htm)

Hegel's abstractions become legible once three ideas are in place: reason lives in a community and has a history; anything defined against its opposite turns out to contain it; and so a single community can split in two and destroy itself, which is what Hegel sees in _Antigone_.

## Three ideas behind the language

### 1. Reason is social and historical

For much of the philosophy before Hegel, reason sits in the individual mind and is the same everywhere and always. For Hegel, reason exists first in shared forms of life: customs, laws, institutions, the things a people takes for granted as right. He calls this shared, living reason _Geist_, "Spirit" (Baillie writes "Mind").

The reading says this directly. Spirit "maintains itself by being reflected in the minds of the component individuals," and "preserves them within itself"; as "actual substance, that spirit is a Nation (_Volk_); _qua_ concrete consciousness, it is the Citizens of the nation" (§447). Spirit is neither above the citizens nor merely in their heads. It is the shared life that exists only through them.

Spirit also changes. The section ends with the Greek "ethical form […] of the life of spirit" passing away so that "another mode appears in its place" (§476). Each form of shared life is a stage, and its failures move it to the next.

### 2. The logic: A = A, and A ≠ A

Ordinary logic starts from identity: A = A. A thing is simply what it is, and it excludes what it is not.

Hegel's objection is that nothing can be identified that way alone. To say what A is, you must say what it is not; its identity is made out of its relation to its other. So A's being A already includes not-A. In shorthand: A = A, and also A ≠ A, because A is only itself through what it excludes.

That is the engine of dialectic. A position taken by itself, and pushed all the way, reveals that it depends on the thing it denies. The contradiction is internal, not imposed from outside, and it drives the position past itself into something new that keeps what was true in both sides. Hegel's word for this is _Aufhebung_: cancelled, preserved, and lifted up at once. (The formula "thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is not Hegel's own vocabulary and makes the process sound mechanical.)

One sentence compresses the whole movement: an act turns the harmony of the two laws into "a transition of opposites into one another, by which each proves to be the annihilation rather than the confirmation of its self and its opposite" (§464).

### 3. One Spirit divided

Put the first two ideas together and Hegel's reading of the play follows. Creon and Antigone are not two people with two opinions. They are the two halves of one Spirit, each acting as if it were the whole. The tragedy is the city discovering, through them, that it contains its own opposite. The outline below traces that movement through the section.

## Outline: one Spirit dividing against itself

1. **One Spirit (§§446–47).** Greek ethical life is a single shared world whose unity is "immediate": unreflective, lived rather than examined.
2. **It divides (§§446–53).** "The simple substance of spirit […] divides itself into parts": a law of universality and a law of individuality, the city and the family, human law and divine law. Yet "each of these areas […] remains, however, spirit in its entirety" (§446). Each half is the whole community seen from one side.
3. **Each half depends on the other (§§454–60).** "Neither of the two is alone self-complete" (§460). The city is A; the family is its not-A, and also its condition.
4. **Action makes the contradiction explicit (§§463–71).** In peaceful times the two complement each other, "a world without blot or stain" (§463). When someone acts wholly from one side, the hidden dependence becomes open war.
5. **Victory becomes defeat (§§472–75).** The city's triumph over the family "learns that its supreme right is supreme wrong, its victory rather its own defeat" (§474).
6. **The form of life dissolves (§476)** and gives way to another.

## Glossary

|Hegel's word|Read it as|
|---|---|
|Spirit / Mind (_Geist_)|A community's shared, living way of knowing and valuing|
|Substance|That shared life considered as given and inherited: custom, what is simply done|
|Ethical (_sittlich_)|Belonging to customary social life, not "moral" in the modern private sense|
|Immediate|Unreflective, taken for granted, not yet examined|
|In itself / for itself|Implicit, potential / explicit, aware of itself (both appear in §447)|
|Moment|An aspect of a whole, not a point in time|
|Negation, the negative|The work of limiting and distinguishing; not "bad"|
|Universal / particular / individual|The community's law / this group or role / this person|
|Human law|The city's law: public, known to all, embodied in government (§448)|
|Divine law|The family's law: unwritten, tied to the dead and the underworld (§§449–52)|
