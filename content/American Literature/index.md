# Foundations of American Literature Fall 2026

Prof. Modestino

Do we still read closely? 

Sometimes it can feel like the whole world wants to deprive us of the care, intensity, and sustained attention reading calls on us to perform. The attention economy breaks down our resistance, our habits, our discipline, and screams in our face, “look at this”, “care about that”, “be outraged”, “be anxious”, “be afraid,” and our daily lives are overstuffed with urgency and stress. Habits of reading and study under duress and interruption can become pragmatic: “what does this mean?” “What’s the message?” “What do I need for a grade or other goal?” And yet, many of us dream of more time to read and attend with care: for pleasure, for political and cultural insight, for self-growth, for community. We want to engage deeply with the beauty of language, the struggle and joy of self-expression, the labor of insight and critical thinking about the cultures surrounding us.  Everywhere today, people worry if they or others are not reading enough.

The wager of this course is that Early American Literature has a great deal to teach us about why we care about reading, how we can be better readers, the deeper beliefs that fuel reading, and social and political anxieties about reading. From Puritans to Transcendentalists and Abolitionists, from preachers of an angry God to the fiery prophecies of Frederick Douglass, from narrators of exemplary lives to poets of injustice, reading, focus, and the problem of interpretation have driven some of the most powerful imaginative works between 1620 and 1865. This course will read Early American Literature to take reading seriously in three core ways:

**First**: Reading is a practice, a habit of attention and interpretation that can be focused on texts, but also, analogically, on history and the natural world. We will think about how diverse groups translated practices originally aimed at Biblical interpretation to areas well beyond scripture.

**Second:** Reading has theological and metaphysical significance. People in Early America did not think of reading as merely an enjoyable activity, but as something of great spiritual, moral, and, by extension, political significance. We will take these beliefs seriously and try to think hard about ways we might inherit them despite a contemporary secular framework that demotes the significance of our activities to mere mechanism and calculation or leisure and consumption.

**Third:** Reading often happens alone, but the practices and beliefs surrounding reading are communal and historical, and the practice of reading has historical and communal effects. Reading has mobilized religious visionaries and political utopians. It has brought coherence to certain myths of America and offered openings in those myths of consensus for dissent and protest. Sometimes it has done both at once, producing consensus by the ritualization of dissent—an important mode that, as we will see, originated with the Puritans.

Do we still read closely? By December you may not have an answer, but you will have spent a semester in the company of writers who thought the question mattered enormously — and you will be asked, more than once, what you've inherited from that conviction, even in a world that has largely stopped believing reading can save anyone
